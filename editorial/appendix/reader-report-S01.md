### Chapter 1 ch 1
- **Dateline as written:** "6:00 AM 14th February 2010, Girbin, Scotland" (ch 1)
- **POV:** An omniscient narrator. It starts panoramic, moves to Daniel Roberto, then to his unnamed daughter.
- **Setting:** A small town in Scotland (called a "town" and a "hamlet") on a Sunday morning in winter. The town square, the church and the family home.
- **Beats:**
  - A misty, uneasy dawn. The narrator insists that "it had that uneasiness" (ch 1).
  - A black Range Rover with tinted glass rolls up and parks near the town square. It stays there, "blending with the surroundings" (ch 1). The next paragraph calls it a "Land Rover" (ch 1).
  - The town wakes: the shops open, the church bell rings, people go to mass (ch 1).
  - Daniel Roberto is a deliveryman and a devoted family man. His Sunday ritual is muffins from the bakery and a full breakfast (ch 1).
  - His early-teen daughter plays a private game. She watches him leave from the window and then pretends to be asleep when he comes back (ch 1).
  - The narrator gives a heavy-handed warning: "little they knew that this Sunday would change their lives forever. Change for the worse." (ch 1)
- **What the reader learns:** A loving family, a sinister car and some impending harm. We do not yet know that the daughter is our heroine.
- **Ending hook:** An omniscient "little they knew" line. It is a cliché, and the chapter ends *before* anything happens. The car and the father never meet on the page.

## 3. Continuity, logic & factual errors

### A. Geography: this is critical and appears in every dateline
1. **"Stockholm, Denmark"** appears in the datelines [ch 2, ch 3, ch 4, ch 6, ch 7]. Stockholm is the capital of Sweden. The text calls the place a "small Danish town" (ch 2) and a "burg", and later chapters say "before coming to Denmark". So the author means a small Danish town, not Stockholm. **Fix:** Use a real or fictional Danish town about 40 minutes by train from Copenhagen. **Helsingør** (Elsinore) fits almost perfectly: it is 45–50 minutes by train, and it has 16th-century royal and castle associations, which suits the "16th-century palace" hotel. Change all datelines through the book (there are 7 in total) to match.
2. **The train to Copenhagen takes "about forty minutes"** (ch 3). That is impossible from Stockholm, which is 5+ hours away. From Helsingør it is correct.
3. **A tram** to the station [ch 3, ch 7]. Small Danish towns had no trams in 2014; Denmark's only tram lines, in Aarhus and Odense, opened in 2017 and 2022. **Fix:** Use a bus, or have her walk.
4. **"Platform F"** (ch 3). Danish stations number their tracks (spor 1, 2…). **Fix:** "track 2", or cut it.
5. **Copenhagen → Basel → a "detour at Zurich"** [ch 7, Ch5]. Any train from Denmark reaches **Basel before Zurich**. Basel is the border city, so a Basel-bound train never passes through Zurich. As written, she could not have got off "early" at Zurich. **Fix:** Route her via Hamburg on the overnight *CityNightLine* sleeper (Hamburg–Basel–Zurich, which ran in 2014). She buys a ticket to Basel but stays aboard to Zurich. That makes the "detour" real and plausible. Verify the 2014 timetable before committing.
6. **"downtown facing view"** [ch 3, ch 7]. This is an Americanism, and odd for a small Danish town. Use "a view over the old town" or "over the harbour".

### B. Timeline and dateline contradictions
7. **The Ch4 dateline "12:00 AM 12th April"** (ch 4) is midnight at the *start* of 12 April, 19 hours before she even meets Benedikt. The events it covers happen around 2:00–3:00 AM on **13 April**. **Fix:** "2:00 AM 13th April 2014".
8. **Arrival in Zurich at 4:20 AM on 13 April** ch 5 is impossible. She left the hotel about 2:30 AM (ch 6), waited 15 minutes at the Carson Hotel (ch 7), took a tram, rode 40 minutes to Copenhagen and waited up to an hour (ch 3). The earliest she could leave Copenhagen is about 5 AM, and Copenhagen to Zurich is 13–15 hours by rail. **Fix:** With the night-train route in item 5, arrival is the morning of 14 April. Or she flies, but then the train-name evidence fails.
9. **"Leaving in half hour"** (ch 3) vs "After an hour-long wait" (ch 3). These contradict each other. Pick one.
10. **2:00 → 2:20 → 2:30 AM** [ch 3, ch 6]. Between waking "past 2:00" and 2:20 she changes clothes, picks a briefcase, photographs a paper, searches for the wallet, and spends "about fifteen minutes" cutting glass. Then within ten minutes she climbs down two floors, nearly falls, goes up a rope, changes, grabs her bag and is in the lobby by 2:30. **Fix:** Have her wake at "a little after one", and have the bellman put her departure at "a little after three".
11. **"The last four years of her meticulous planning"** (ch 2) vs "For the past two years, she had visualized being here" (ch 3) vs "she came to this place twice before" (ch 7) vs "Ayr for the past two years" (book.md line 333). Four years fits 2010→2014, and two years fits the Ayr period. **Fix:** Make it "four years of planning, two of them spent circling this hotel", or harmonise both to one number.
12. **Her age.** "early teens" in 2010 (ch 1) and "late teens" in 2014 (ch 2). Later the book calls her a "twenty-something girl" (book.md line 1111) and gives her a corporate job, a year of physical training and two years in Ayr. **Fix:** Make her clearly an adult in 2014 (20–22). That means "fifteen" or "sixteen" in Ch1, or moving Ch1 back to 2008. This is not only continuity: see section 5 on the sex scene.

### C. Poker: the hand logic is broken in both hands
The format is described as five-card draw or stud with antes plus "blind" betting before players look at their cards ch 2. That is an eccentric house variant, but workable if it is flagged as such. There is no draw round, and no one exchanges cards. Seating (from ch 2): dealer, then Robinson (to the dealer's left), Andrea, Alberto, Benedikt, Holger.

13. **Hand 1: Alberto's straight flush is impossible** (ch 2). Alberto shows 7♠ and 10♠. The only straight flushes containing both are 6-7-8-9-10♠ and 7-8-9-10-J♠, and both **require the 8♠ and 9♠**. But "Benedikt had 8 and 9 of spades". Only one deck is in play ("The first pack of the card was dealt" (ch 2)). Both men cannot hold those cards.
    - Benedikt's hand is listed as "Two aces" plus 8♠ 9♠, which is **four cards**. The fifth is missing.
    - The narration is also out of order. "Alberto opened the rest of the three cards and took the game away. Benedikt had 8 and 9 of spades…" reveals Benedikt's cards after the winner is announced.
    - **Fix:** Give Benedikt A♥ A♦ 8♣ 9♦ 2♣ (a pair of aces). Give Alberto 6♠ 7♠ 8♠ 9♠ 10♠, a straight flush, or a plain flush if you want it less far-fetched. Show both hands before the winner is called.
14. **Hand 2: Benedikt should lose** (ch 2). Benedikt has 5♥ 6♣ 7♣ 8♦ 9♥, a nine-high **straight**. Alberto has "his two kings … one more king and a pair of a queen", which is K-K-K-Q-Q, a **full house**. A full house beats a straight. As written, the climax of the chapter goes to the wrong player, and any poker-literate agent or reader will stop reading here.
    - **Fix:** Keep Rita's read ("He has the cards, but not high in the order"). Give Alberto **three kings** (K-K-K-Q-4) or **two pair**, kings and queens (K-K-Q-Q-4). Either loses to a straight, and three kings matches "half bluff" best. Delete "and a pair of a queen", or change it to "and a lone queen".
15. **The pot and stacks don't add up** ch 2. Every player starts with £10M (ch 2). In hand 1 Benedikt lost roughly £3.4M (a 200k raise, then 1M, then a 2M re-raise over Alberto's 1.2M), so he has about £6.5M left. In an all-in he can only win what he puts in, matched from each caller. His winnable pot is roughly £6.5M from each of himself and Alberto, plus Andrea's roughly £5–6M, plus about £1.5M each from Holger and Robinson. That is about £20–22M, not "over whopping thirty-five million" (ch 2).
    - Alberto, who *won* hand 1 and has about £13.5M, would have chips left, so "Alberto out of the game" (ch 2) is wrong.
    - **Fix:** Either (a) raise the buy-in to £25M, or (b) make the pot "nearly twenty million" and have Benedikt's all-in *cover* Alberto because Benedikt won the earlier hands. The second fits "He had won five out of the last seven outings" better.
16. **Hand-1 betting holes.** In round one (ch 2) Robinson, Andrea and Alberto never call Benedikt's raise to 200k before the round closes. In round two, "Alberto raised the stake to one million and two hundred thousand" (ch 2) is a raise of only 200k over Benedikt's 1M. "Pot size nearing two million" (ch 2) is actually about £2.4M+. These are minor, but tidy them or state less arithmetic.
17. **Pounds sterling at a Danish casino** (ch 2). It is odd but defensible for an international private game. Consider euros, or a line saying the game is "played in sterling, as it always had been".
18. **"The En Welch clock rhymed"** (ch 2) is garbled. It should be the **E. N. Welch** clock (a 19th-century American clockmaker), and it "chimed". Suggest: "The E. N. Welch clock on the mantel chimed nine."
19. **"Blind" betting** [ch 2]. Blinds are forced bets in Hold'em. Here the word means betting before looking. If you keep it, gloss it once ("the house tradition of betting blind before the cards were seen").

### D. Drinks, brands and objects
20. **"Krupnik with vodka … a Lithuania delight"** [ch 2]. Krupnik (Lithuanian *krupnikas*) is a honey liqueur *made with* vodka or spirit, so "with vodka" is redundant, and the adjective is "Lithuanian". **Fix:** "A Krupnikas for the lady … A Lithuanian delight." (Krupnik is also Polish, so a Lithuanian-born Benedikt could claim it, which is a nice character hook.)
21. **"vodka with two olives on the side"** (ch 2). Olives mark a vodka martini. Say "a vodka martini, two olives".
22. **"Between the Sheets"** (ch 3) is a real cocktail (cognac, rum, triple sec, lemon), so that is fine. But then "He was still working on his vodka" (ch 3). Did he make himself a vodka as well? One clause fixes it.
23. **"tux" with a "buttoned-down shirt"** (ch 2). A button-down collar is never worn with a dinner jacket. Say "a dress shirt". Also, he arrived in a cobalt Versace suit (ch 2) and changed upstairs. That is fine, but say so.
24. **"rich Danish coffee"** (ch 2) is not a thing. Say "strong Danish coffee", or cut it.
25. **CCTV on "the CD"** watched by switching "the television to AV mode" [ch 7]. In 2014 a hotel's 72 hours of multi-camera footage (ch 7) would be on a DVR or network video system and exported to a laptop or USB drive. It would not fit on a CD, and "tapes" [ch 7] is also outdated. **Fix:** "Damian plugged a laptop into the television".
26. **"ten Kronor"** (ch 3). The Danish currency is kroner (kronor is Swedish), and 10 DKK is about £1, an insultingly small tip at 2:30 AM. Make it "a hundred kroner".
27. **A 16th-century palace with 15+ floors and "high-speed" elevators** [ch 2, ch 3]. These are incompatible. **Fix:** Put the executive suite in a modern tower wing ("the new wing rose fifteen floors behind the old palace"), or lower the suite to the 5th floor. The later line about "jump out of the 15th floor" (ch 6) then changes too.
28. **Morning light at 6:00 AM in Scotland in mid-February** ch 1. Sunrise is around 7:45–8:00 AM, so "bright dawn sun" and "bright sun" (ch 1) are wrong. The bakery run at 6 AM on a Sunday and crowds at mass before 7 AM are also implausible. **Fix:** A dateline of 8:30 AM. (14 Feb 2010 *was* a Sunday, and 12 April 2014 *was* a Saturday, so those check out.)
29. **Peanut butter sandwiches and muffins** as a Scottish Sunday breakfast (ch 1) read American. Consider "rolls" or "morning rolls", plus bacon or tattie scones if you want local colour.

### E. Plot holes and unmotivated actions
30. **The sedative is never planted.** "The sedative lotion she applied on him worked" (ch 3) comes from nowhere, and later the doctor says it was "applied to your lips" (ch 7). If it was on *her* lips, she would be drugged too. **Fix:** One sentence in (ch 3), e.g. "Before leaving the bathroom she smoothed the balm over her fingertips…", and the report says "absorbed through the skin". Or she tips it into his vodka and the doctor says "in your drink".
31. **She leaves her gown and her "left-over" in his bathroom** [ch 3]. Benedikt searches the suite [ch 6] and says "nothing was missing", but never finds the evidence she left behind. That gown is a clue a security team would trace, since it is custom-made with sewn-in compartments. **Fix:** Either she takes it (rolled into her bodysuit pack) or Benedikt finds it and it becomes a lead.
32. **Fingerprint report "negative"** (ch 7). No fingerprinting is shown, and she touched glasses, the robe and the bar *before* putting on gloves (ch 3). **Fix:** "Her prints were everywhere and matched nothing on any database." That is more chilling and more accurate.
33. **The combination briefcase is picked with a hairpin** (ch 3). Benedikt then opens it with "the number combination" (ch 6). You cannot pick a dial combination lock with a hairpin. "It didn't buzz" is also meaningless. **Fix:** She shims the latch with a thin blade from the gown, or she watched him dial it earlier.
34. **"Rita" vs "Sanchez" identity** [ch 7, ch 7]. If she registered as Rita with a fake address, where does a "photo ID" saying Sanchez come from at a train station? Danish domestic trains do not check ID or record names. Likewise the name "Trevor" for a fellow passenger (ch 7) could not be retrieved. **Fix:** Move the Sanchez reveal to Benedikt's passport-office contact, which the book already sets up (book.md line 333). Have Damian find the watcher only as "the same man in a dark windcheater on the platform cameras at both stations". "Trevor" can then come from the international sleeper reservation manifest, which is plausible for a CityNightLine berth.
35. **Benedikt's rant: "she wandered into his bedroom"** (ch 6). He invited her in and dismissed the guards (ch 3). **Fix:** Make it self-reproach: "…and he had led her there himself."
36. **Why cut glass at all?** The guarded corridor is a reason (ch 3), but it is never stated. One line would do: "The corridor would be full of his men; the window was the only door."
37. **The decoy theft vs the cut window** [ch 3, ch 6]. Taking cash to "misguide the future investigation" is undercut by leaving a professionally removed pane. It is not fatal, since Benedikt sees through it (ch 6), which is good. But Rita's reasoning should acknowledge the contradiction, or the pane should be less obviously professional.
38. **Phone discipline.** She uses a public payphone for security (ch 3) but then calls on her mobile in Zurich (ch 5). Later the book says she and Stephen "had set a protocol not to call" (book.md line 319), which contradicts both calls. **Fix:** Keep the payphone call to a third party (the gruff man), and cut the mobile call in (ch 5). Stephen is already waiting as arranged.
39. **The watcher knows her route** (ch 4) ("He knew the layout of the floor well … certain of her breakout route"). This implies inside knowledge of her plan. It is a strong hook if paid off later; confirm that the book explains it. If not, soften it to "he had watched her rig the rope that afternoon".
40. **The watcher hears "a soft tinkling"** (ch 4) but doesn't register the loud bird commotion (ch 3), which in Rita's version woke the guest below. Align the two versions.
41. **"French window" / "lancet"** (ch 4). Her room window is variously a pane she cuts, a window she opens from a rope, a "French window" and a "lancet". A lancet is a narrow gothic arch that you can't climb through easily. Choose one.
42. **Poker room location.** She "sauntered towards the casino" on foot (ch 2), but Benedikt's CCTV shows her "approaching the elevators to the first floor where the poker tournament was held" (ch 7). Pick one.
43. **Sofitel vs Carson Hotel** [ch 3, ch 7]. Rita "took another taxi" from the Sofitel, and Damian says she crossed the street to the Carson Hotel for it. That is compatible, but tell it once, in a way that doesn't spoil the other version.
44. **"She came to this place twice before … always stayed in the same room"** (ch 7), yet at the door she claims she is "visiting the casino for the first time" (ch 2). That is fine as a lie. But hotel staff and Benedikt's security, who vet everyone around him, would have flagged a young woman booking the same room under his floor at three tournaments running. Benedikt might remark on this lapse.
45. **The doctor's blood result arrives within about two hours on a Sunday morning** [ch 6, ch 7]. This is possible with private money, but you could say "a private lab, paid to hurry".
46. **"Basel wasn't a big town"** (ch 7). It has about 170,000 people. Say "Basel was not a large city".
47. **Stephen is absent from Ch1** ch 1, though the later backstory depends on "Mom, dad, brother and me". Plant the brother with one clause at the breakfast table.
48. **"Mission successfully accomplished"** (ch 5) vs the later "four long rumpus nights" (book.md line 319). The mission here is one night. Harmonise.

## 4. Pacing & flow

- **Ch1 is a mood-only prologue with no event.** It is 5 paragraphs of weather and routine, then "little they knew". The Range Rover never touches the father. Agents read openings for *event*. **Recommendation:** End the chapter with one concrete, strange image: the father doesn't come back from the bakery; the muffins are on the pavement; the Range Rover is gone. Cut roughly 40% of the weather in (ch 1). Plant the brother.
- **Ch2 opening repeats a beat.** Benedikt "vanished behind the lift doors" (ch 2), and then in (ch 2) "The man she was waiting for was walking towards her. He swiftly went past her to enter the elevator bank." The same entrance is narrated twice. Then he reappears at the casino door (ch 2) with no mention of coming back down. **Fix:** Cut the second pass in (ch 2) and add "Twenty minutes later he came down again, changed…" before (ch 2).
- **The poker scene is betting-by-numbers.** ch 2 and ch 2 are running totals ("raised by three million… three and a half million"). A reader can't follow them and doesn't care. The tension should sit in Rita's reading of Alberto's tells, which is the best idea in the chapter. **Recommendation:** Keep one or two exact figures per hand (the opening stake and the final pot). Summarise the rest, and give the saved space to Rita's observations and Benedikt's unreadable reactions.
- **Ch3 rushes where it should linger and lingers where it could rush.** The seduction is compressed into two paragraphs [ch 3], with an abrupt push-away and then instant sex. The briefcase and the photographs, which are the heist's actual *point*, get three sentences (ch 3), and the map is only named later as "I have the map" (ch 3). Then the train logistics ch 3 are itemised. **Recommendation:** Slow down on the briefcase, the cylinder and the paper (a glimpse of the map itself is a hook). Compress the taxi, tram and train chain to one paragraph.
- **The escape route is told three times.** It appears as action in Ch3 ch 3, as observation in Ch4 ch 4, and as investigation in Ch7 ch 7]. The Ch7 investigation reads as filler because the reader already knows every answer. **Recommendation:** In Ch3, keep Rita's route vague ("she changed taxis twice and doubled back on foot") so Benedikt's deductions in Ch7 are *discoveries* for the reader too.
- **Ch4 spoils Ch5.** Ch4 tells us a man is following her. That flattens the Ch5 ending ("we are being followed" and the grey Peugeot), which should be the section's best shock. **Recommendation:** Either cut Ch4 to a single atmospheric paragraph that doesn't show him following her to the train, or fold Ch4 into Ch5 as the reveal after the Peugeot appears.
- **Ch6 and Ch7 could merge** into one tighter "morning after" chapter. Ch6 ends on logistics with no hook, and Ch7 opens on more logistics. Benedikt gives the same orders twice ("find everything about her" ch 6, then Damian re-delegates them (ch 7)). Merged and cut by about 30%, this would move well.
- **(ch 7) undoes its own suspense.** "little he knew about the detour she took at Zurich" repeats what Ch5 showed and reuses the Ch1 formula. **Recommendation:** Cut it, and end Ch7 on (ch 7)'s admission that he wants to see her, or on the higher-ups' ultimatum.
- **No info-dump yet,** which is to the author's credit. But the absence of *any* stakes about the map in this section (what it is, why it matters) means that by the end of Ch7 the reader has had a heist without knowing the prize. One line from Benedikt in Ch6 ("If that paper has been seen…") would supply the stakes.

## 5. Prose problems (section-specific)

**a) Misused or near-miss words (malapropisms).** This is the single biggest prose issue in the section.
- "A sight no matter how many times she had seen never failed to **cease** her excitement." (ch 1). It means the opposite of what is intended. Fix: "never failed to thrill her".
- "There was a strong **rumor** about both father and daughter playing this little game" (ch 1). Fix: "It was an open secret…".
- "she understood impassiveness was the **unwarranted** protocol of the game" (ch 2). Fix: "unwritten".
- "which was quite evident in his fidgety **stature**" (ch 2). See also "recovered from his lost **stature**" (ch 7). Fix: "posture" / "composure".
- "She **wielded** from her repose" (ch 5). Fix: "She woke".
- "continued her conversation with an **embodied** tone" (ch 3). Fix: "a low, even tone".
- "I am not a fool to get **winded** in her deception" (ch 6). Fix: "taken in by".
- "He saw how Alberto approached her and the way she **dour** him." (ch 7). Fix: "rebuffed him".
- "his mind had morphed into a **confetto**" (ch 7). Fix: "a jumble".
- "in his most **uncanny** way, he uttered" (ch 2). Fix: "in his most matter-of-fact way".
- "from the executive floor's **badested ridge**" (ch 4). Fix: "balustraded ledge".
- "The temperature had **stooped** to single digits" (ch 4). Fix: "dropped".
- "windshield jacket" (ch 4) should be "windcheater"; "taxa" (ch 4) should be "taxi"; "Creek, creek!!" (ch 3) should be "Kraa, kraa!"; "Coming right way" (ch 2) should be "right away".
- "looked **through** her" [ch 2, ch 2]. "Looking through" someone means ignoring them. The author means "into her" or "held her gaze".

**b) Purple or overwrought description.** This is concentrated in chapter openings.
- "the sunbeam was forming a tincture passing through the morning haar. The bright dawn sun was camouflaging in the cold wintery brume as if this was the game it had chosen to play." (ch 1)
- "it appeared like a beast wanting to tame the wilderness of the street" (ch 1)
- "now remodeled as a luxury grandiose" (ch 2); "engulfed in layers of some uncalled mist" (ch 2)
- "the flutter of avian species moving away in agony" (ch 3)
- "raising the mercury to the height of insanity" (ch 2)

**c) Sexualised description.** An agent *will* flag this, especially given her stated age.
- "Her back was exposed to the deep … her full breasts were tantalizing with the top slightly covered by her turquoise blue scarf … her radiant face could make anyone go weak on knees." (ch 2). This is a male-gaze catalogue in what is meant to be *her* POV scene, of a character described in the same paragraph as "in her late teens".
- "There was hunger, filthiness, passion, and desire." (ch 3)
- "He ogled her for few seconds. Long enough to arouse her. With passion and lust, his tongue started exploring her body inch by inch. … she submitted herself to his unfathomable desires. They made love again and again like there was no tomorrow." (ch 3)
- **Fix:** Make her unambiguously adult (see item 12). Describe the gown by what it *hides* (the sewn-in kit), which is plot and not ogling. Keep the sex scene short and in her strategic POV (what it costs her, what it enables), and cut "filthiness" and "ogled".

**d) Head-hopping within scenes.**
- In (ch 3), Rita is nervous, and then "Benedikt hardly consider her any threat".
- In (ch 3), Rita is "taken by surprise", and then "His emotions were a mix of dejection and surprise".
- In (ch 3), the guest below is given "apprehension he may had".
- In (ch 7), the hotel manager "couldn't muster enough courage".
- In (ch 7), "Damian almost jumped out of his skin".
- **Fix:** One POV per scene. Show others' feelings from outside ("his face fell").

**e) Clichés and stock phrases.** Several of these recur.
- "little they knew … Change for the worse" (ch 1); "little he knew" (ch 7)
- "Her heart leaped into her mouth" (ch 3); "like there was no tomorrow" (ch 3); "like pieces of a puzzle coming together" (ch 2); "the cat and the mouse game" (ch 7)
- "sprinted to action" [ch 2, ch 2]; "in a jiffy" [ch 3, ch 7]; "Without wasting any (further) time" [ch 5, ch 6, ch 7]; "put a cursory glance" [ch 3, ch 7]
- "transient repose" / "repose" [ch 1, ch 3, ch 5]
- "failed to comprehend anything meaningful" / "conclude anything meaningful" [ch 4, ch 5]. This one recurs throughout the book.

**f) Dialogue tags and speech verbs.**
- "blurted" is used for calm or ordinary speech [ch 3, ch 5, ch 6, ch 7].
- Others: "gently asserted" (ch 2); "reciprocated introducing himself in a brief syllable 'Benedikt'" (ch 2) (Benedikt has three syllables); "she rejoinders" (ch 2); "Damian gravelly tone" (ch 7).
- Several lines lack end punctuation before the tag, e.g. `"I deeply appreciate your kind gesture" She took…` (ch 2).
- Double exclamation marks: "dames!!" (ch 2), "Pounds!!" (ch 2), "Creek, creek!!" (ch 3).

**g) Garbled dialogue.**
- "I like when someone put their mind to work, especially like me" (ch 2). Suggest: "I like a woman who puts her mind to work. Especially on my behalf."
- "your drink dames!!" (ch 2). Suggest: "Your drink, madam."
- "Report back in twenty minutes. Will you?" (ch 6)

**h) Tense slips and grammar.**
- "He **puts** the spread on the table" (ch 1); "she **rejoinders**" (ch 2); "Benedikt hardly **consider**" (ch 3); "made her **fell** on his lap" (ch 3); "without **no** trace" (ch 6); "couldn't abstain from **not** praising her" (ch 7)
- Missing articles throughout: "few feet", "few seconds", "for good fifteen minutes".

**i) Telling instead of showing.**
- (ch 1) is an entire paragraph of summary virtues ("As a family, they cheered for each other, celebrated every little joy, and lived contented").
- (ch 7): "He was certain it was a well thought out intrusion, planned with extreme measures and executed with brilliance."
- (ch 7): "It was evident he was getting drawn to her".

**j) Narrative Americanisms in a British/Danish setting.**
- "Caucasian male" (ch 2) reads like police-report language. "downtown" [ch 3, ch 7], "pajamas" (ch 7), "cab" alternating with "taxi".
- **Fix:** Choose a British register consistently, since the book is set in Scotland and Europe.

## 6. Strengths

- **The father–daughter window game** (ch 1) is specific, tender and original. It is the emotional seed of the whole revenge plot. "She grabbed her arm sleeves, stretched the cloth further out and used it as a perfect tool for cleaning the haze." This is a real, observed detail. Protect it, and make it the image that returns at the climax.
- **Rita's entrance at the door** ch 2. She engineers a scene so that *he* rescues *her*. This is smart, active and in character, and "She is with me" is a clean, confident beat.
- **"For tonight, let me be your lady luck."** (ch 2) It is a good line: flirtatious, strategic and ironic, since she is anything but his luck.
- **The tell-reading device** [ch 2, ch 2. Rita's first read is *wrong* ("I think he is bluffing", and he has a straight flush), and her humiliation makes the second read (the order of the leg-cross and the left-over-right) pay off. This is a genuine setup and payoff that characterises her as an observer, which is exactly what a heroine-spy needs. Once the card logic is fixed, it is the section's best scene idea.
- **The decoy theft and Benedikt seeing through it** [ch 3, ch 6: "This is a disguise, a trap to make us believe she was here for the money." The antagonist is intelligent, which lifts the book above a standard chase.
- **The near-fall on the facade** (ch 3). Birds bursting from a ledge, a light snapping on below, a hinge giving way. It is a well-conceived suspense set-piece that only needs cleaner prose.
- **The taxi chain as tradecraft** [ch 3, ch 7]. Benedikt's "Sofitel wasn't her destination" is a satisfying detective beat, and it would be more satisfying if Ch3 didn't pre-reveal it.
- **Benedikt's reluctant admiration** [ch 7]: "The girl who had put his entire morning in disarray was not frustrating him for her misdeed, but for her disappearance." This is a nicely turned line and sets up the ambivalent hunter–hunted dynamic.
- **The hint of past trouble** (ch 7): "He was never a philanderer, and once when he tried, he had put himself in trouble." It is economical, intriguing backstory.
- **The Ch5 ending** ch 5. "Mission successfully accomplished" is immediately deflated by "I have a hunch, we are being followed." It is a good reversal and would land harder without Ch4.
- **The structure of datelines with a POV alternation** between hunter and hunted is the right architecture for this kind of thriller. It just needs accurate datelines.

## 7. Honest assessment

As a query sample, these opening chapters would very likely be declined on the first pages. The first obstacle is geographical: "Stockholm, Denmark" sits in the second dateline, repeated five times, and tells an agent that the manuscript hasn't been fact-checked. Then comes a slow, eventless prologue in ornate, often misused vocabulary ("never failed to cease her excitement", "a tincture passing through the morning haar"). If the agent reaches Chapter 2, the poker scene, which is meant to showcase the heroine's intelligence, ends with a straight beating a full house after a straight flush that needs cards the other player holds. Any reader who knows poker stops trusting the author there. The sexualised description of a heroine the text itself calls "in her late teens" is a further, serious red flag in today's market. Underneath all this is a genuinely commercial set-up: a wronged daughter who spends four years engineering a honey-trap heist on a powerful man; a smart antagonist who sees through her decoy and is drawn to her anyway; a third-party shadow; and a revenge motive rooted in a vivid, tender family image. The bones are sound, and the heist-then-chase shape is exactly what thriller editors buy.

**The single most important fix:** make the opening *factually trustworthy*. Correct the geography (a real Danish town about 40 minutes from Copenhagen, and a rail route on which Zurich plausibly follows Basel). Fix the datelines and the timeline. Rebuild both poker hands so the cards and the pot are legal. Most readers will forgive ornate prose in a debut. They will not forgive a thriller that gets its own mechanics wrong in chapter two. After that, the next priorities are the malapropisms and making the heroine clearly an adult, in that order.
