# Style Report 03: Dialogue

Scope: I read S03 (ch16–23), S06 (ch41–49) and S07 (ch50–56) in full, and every paragraph in the rest of the book that contains a quotation mark (743 dialogue paragraphs in total). Counts come from scripted searches of `paras.json` / `book.md` and are approximate: tag detection only looks at the narration right next to a quote. Paragraph IDs are given as [P###].

---

## 0. Summary for the editor

1. **Dialogue tags are the most distinctive part of the author's style and also the biggest problem.** The book has about 424 speech-verb tags. Only about 16 of them are *said* or *asked*. The rest are *continued* (66), *blurted* (51), *spoke* (36), *stated* (31), *announced* (30), *responded* (23), *enquired* (15), *mumbled* (13) and so on. The variety is part of the voice. Keep some of it, fix the misused verbs, and cut the overused ones by about two-thirds.
2. **The same sentence frame, "with a [adjective] tone/voice", appears about 147 times in dialogue.** Many are contradictory ("with a passive voice blurted" (ch 21)), misused ("with a frustrating tone" (ch 43)), or doubled up with a loud verb. Allow at most one colouring per speech act.
3. **Mechanical punctuation errors are frequent and simple to fix.** Examples: missing commas before the closing quote (about 50), tag questions split into two sentences (8), "!!" (12), straight and curly quotes mixed in 111 paragraphs, missing opening quotes on continued paragraphs (11 multi-paragraph speeches), and zero-width spaces (12).
4. **Every character speaks the same formal, uncontracted English.** Contractions run at about 15 per 1,000 dialogue words for *every* speaker (Benedikt 16, Sanchez 17, Maxwell 20, Stephen 14). Uncontracted forms run 2–3 times higher. "Let's" appears 0 times and "Let us" 19 times. Differentiation should come mainly from contraction policy, with one or two small markers per character. Do not add new slang.
5. **The Portuguese contains several real errors**, and some other foreign details are wrong: "Kein Ausgang", "sotto voice", the Danish "Hej" in "Stockholm, Denmark", and "twenty pesos" in Portugal. See §5.

### 1.2 Policy: which tags stay

The author's variety is a genuine habit and part of the book's texture. Replacing everything with *said* would make it read like someone else's book. Apply the following rules.

**Rule T1: Fix every misuse.** These are errors, not style: *hushed* (as a speech verb), *blazoned*, *rejoinders*, *imposed a question*, *clamored*, *spoke* directly before a quote, *stated* for a question, and *blurted/announced* where the speaker is calm or whispering.

**Rule T2: Default to *said* or *asked*, or to no tag at all.** In a two-person exchange, after the first two lines, drop tags entirely. The author already does this well in places (Benedikt and Damian in ch 21; the Tallinn lunch ch 49).

**Rule T3: Keep one "colour" verb per scene, not per line.** In each chapter allow at most about two of the author's signature verbs (*blurted, announced, stated, declared, queried, uttered, put forward*), and choose the ones where the meaning fits.

**Rule T4: Prefer the author's own action beats to substitute verbs.** The author writes good beats ("She took a quick break to chew her bread, gulped some Kali, and took her narration forward" (ch 50); "He poured a glass of orange juice for her and continued further" (ch 49)). If a tag is cut, a nearby beat usually carries the attribution already.

**Rule T5: Never "upgrade" a tag.** Do not replace *blurted* with *quipped*, *breathed*, *hissed*, *rasped*, and so on. The change should always move toward plainer words.

**Rule T6: Keep *continued* and *came the reply*.** They are the author's rhythm. Only remove "further".

Rough targets for the whole book: *blurted* 51→~13, *stated* 31→~8, *announced* 30→~10, *spoke* (before a quote) →0, *said/asked* ~16→~120. Also expect about 100 tags to be deleted outright.

### 2.3 Trimming rule

**Rule A1: One colouring per speech act.** Choose the vivid verb *or* the tone phrase *or* an action beat, never two.

**Rule A2: Delete the tone phrase whenever (a) the quoted words already show the tone, (b) it contradicts the verb, or (c) the adjective is misused.** This covers most of the 147.

**Rule A3: Of the rest, keep about one in three**: the ones that are actually informative and sound like the author on a good day. Good keepers: "in a dead voice" (ch 53); "in a flat tone" (ch 33); "with a flat voice" (ch 48); "in her fading voice" (ch 52); "in a low tone" (ch 9); "He concluded with a shaking voice" (ch 43); "his voice trailed" (ch 16).

**Rule A4: Prefer *voice* for sound (low, flat, shaking) and *tone* for attitude (sarcastic, teasing).** Don't let both appear in one sentence ("in her voice" and "tone" together, (ch 53)).

**Rule A5: Also trim the "long runway" before a quote**, one modifier at most. The author likes "[Adverbial], [adverbial], and with a [adj] tone she [verb], '…'". Example (ch 18): "He didn't greet her, displayed a stern look, and with a demanding tone, he probed why she was frisking around." Keep one of *stern look* / *demanding tone*.

Worked example (ch 2):
- Original: *Though uncomfortable she mustered some courage, and with a muffled tone stated, "I am truly sorry…"*
- Edit: *Though uncomfortable, she mustered some courage. "I am truly sorry…"*

Worked example (ch 2):
- Original: *…Benedikt looked through her and with a possessive voice queried, "May I?"*
- Edit: *…Benedikt looked through her. "May I?"* (The look already shows the possessiveness, and "May I?" is a question.)

### 3.2 Reported and direct speech mixed

Reported speech needs back-shifted tenses and no quotation marks. Direct speech needs quotation marks and a capital letter. Watch for:

- "She still carried her infectious smile and stated she is new to the town and visiting the casino for the first time. "I have the money", were her concluding words." (ch 2). Change to: "…and said she was new in town and visiting the casino for the first time. “I have the money,” she finished."
- "He queried himself, “his operation ‘Sub Rosa’ had nothing to do with Lisbon then why is she traveling to Lisbon?”" (ch 21). This mixes third-person narration inside a first-person quote. Change to: "He asked himself, “Operation Sub Rosa has nothing to do with Lisbon, so why is she going there?”" (or render it as italic thought).
- "declared that there were four John Patterson" (ch 12). Change to "…four John Pattersons".
- "if she refused, to tell the truth, he would be forced" (ch 18). The stray comma turns "to tell the truth" into an idiom. Change to "if she refused to tell the truth".
- "Kosovo expressed his discomfort and stated he was not interested to let Daniel go." (ch 85). Change to "…said he had no intention of letting Daniel go."
- "Benedikt provided a perfect explanation about the choice of the port to Sanchez, "This harbor was selected by design. As we didn't want any undue attention."" (ch 54). This is a quote grafted onto narration and ends in a fragment. Change to: "“This harbor was chosen by design,” Benedikt explained. “We didn’t want any undue attention.”"

### 3.3 Thoughts vs speech

The book puts unspoken thoughts in quotation marks ("“Did she dodge Benedikt? …” Maxwell’s mind was spinning" (ch 52); "“How would she react once she reclaims her senses?…”" (ch 46); "“Have I done a mistake trusting him?…”" (ch 56); ch 67; (ch 30) "…”, he thought"). This makes it hard to tell what is said aloud.

**Rule: thoughts go in italics without quotation marks, in first person and present tense:** *Have I made a mistake trusting him?* Words muttered aloud to oneself keep quotation marks with *murmured* or *mumbled* ([ch 9, ch 22, ch 23]).

### 3.4 House pattern (examples from the book)

1. **Speech, then tag:** comma inside, lowercase tag.
   “Drive as fast as you can,” he told Damian. (ch 21)
2. **Tag, then speech:** comma after the verb, capital first word.
   He said, “Why don’t you leave me alone? You don’t know what you are looking for.” (ch 16)
3. **Question or exclamation, then tag:** no comma, lowercase tag.
   “Can you hear the footsteps?” she screamed. (ch 39)
4. **Speech, then action beat (not a speech verb):** full stop, then a new sentence with a capital.
   “Try me once. I won’t disappoint you.” There was excitement in his voice… (ch 49)
5. **Split sentence:**
   “Sanchez, his real name is Maxwell,” Stephen said, “and he isn’t a bad person.”
6. **Interruption and trailing off:** em dash for interruption, ellipsis for trailing off.
   “Sanchez, his real name is Maxwell and he isn’t a bad person. In fact—”
   “You stay out of this,” Sanchez cut in. (ch 53)
   “Since last night I…” (ch 21)
7. **Tag questions:** comma, lowercase.
   “Leave me alone, will you?” (ch 47) / “You don’t have patience, do you?” (ch 49) / “Let’s get a coffee, shall we?” (ch 18)
8. **Vocatives:** set off with commas, and capitalise "Chief" when it is used as a name.
   “Sorry, Chief. The flight has left.” (ch 21) / “I gave you one job, Francis, and you ruined it.” (ch 28)
9. **Multi-paragraph speech:** an opening quote at the start of every paragraph, and a closing quote only at the end of the last. ch 50, ch 53
10. **Exclamation:** a single "!" only. Use it sparingly in narration ("thirty-five million pounds!!" (ch 2) becomes a plain full stop).
11. **Nested quotes:** single curly quotes, as already used at (ch 50).
12. **Foreign phrase:** italicise the foreign words inside the double quotes. If a translation is given, either (a) keep the author's parenthetical, punctuated consistently: “*Sim.*” (“Yes.”), or (b) let the next line or narration carry the meaning. Pick one method for the whole book (see §5).

### 4.1 The core finding: one register for everyone

Everyone uses the same formal, complete-sentence, uncontracted English, from the Scottish student and her kid brother to the Estonian hotel receptionist, a Portuguese taxi tout and a Russian gangster. The same stock phrases pass between characters:

- "What took you so long?": Benedikt [ch 21, ch 37, ch 38, ch 40] and Stephen (ch 57)

See Benedikt ch 49, Maxwell ch 53 ("You know how I confronted you and put you through the training"), Bradley (ch 18), Stephen (ch 24) ("We are liberal in using our identities for buying airlines and train tickets"), and Jules's map lecture ch 31.

**The low-cost fix is a contraction policy set per character (§4.3).** It separates the voices noticeably without rewriting lines, and it keeps the author's grammar and vocabulary.

### 4.2 Character profiles

**Sanchez (as "Rita" in ch2–3).** She is performing: flirtatious, poised, slightly theatrical. "For tonight, let me be your lady luck." (ch 2); "surprise me." (ch 3); "I deeply appreciate your kind gesture" (ch 2). **The formality works here because it is a mask.** Keep it uncontracted.

**Sanchez (herself).** She is polite and firm and speaks in short, decisive sentences when she is in command ("This is my battle and I cannot let anyone else fight it for me." (ch 50); "I didn’t come here to take your advice. You can either support my decision or we go our own ways." (ch 53)). With Stephen she is bossy and fond ("You are an ingenuous soul." (ch 24); "Stephen, come sit beside me. This place is so pristine…" (ch 26)). Her verbal tics are "I need…", "You need to trust me", and "Please…" when pleading ([ch 15, ch 61]).
- Problem: her 1,600-word life story ch 50 is written narration inside quotation marks. It has no spoken rhythm, and she describes latching doors and pulling stools in procedural detail. The author already switches to reported speech at (ch 50) ("She told him how she started working for TransPacific…"). **Suggestion:** keep the emotional opening ch 50 and the father's remembered words (ch 50) in direct speech. Move the basement and notebook search ch 50 into narration, or break it with one or two of Benedikt's beats. The editorial choice is the author's, but punctuation must be fixed either way.
- She is Scottish (Ayr and "a village three hours away"). Her speech has American words: "Mom" [ch 50, ch 50, ch 53], "soccer ball" (ch 50). **Change these to "Mum" and "football".** This is a correction, not modernisation. "Kirk" (ch 50) is a good Scottish touch; keep it.
- Grammar slips to correct in her speech: "Would you know where in Russia is, he?" (ch 75) → "Do you know where in Russia he is?"; "Are you, all right?" (ch 52) → "Are you all right?"; "I am wrong assuming we have a connection" (ch 69) → "Was I wrong to think we had a connection?"; "Have I done a mistake" (ch 56) → "Have I made a mistake".

**Benedikt.** He is the most distinctive speaker and the easiest to protect. He has two registers:
- **Boss:** clipped commands, then a rhetorical check. "Report back in twenty minutes. Will you?" (ch 6); "Details of the passengers will be sent to you in the next fifteen minutes. Any questions?" (ch 21); "Is that clear?" [ch 6, ch 37]. Also sarcasm: "Good, that must be a long stroll to Noŭka" (ch 76).
- **Courtly lover:** formal, slightly foreign, old-fashioned. "You invaded my privacy, came close to me and made me behave like human again… When you are around, there is calmness. There is serenity." (ch 49); "Let us give each other our hand and let the destiny lead us…" (ch 72); "I will always love you, no matter what happens." (ch 83).
- **Keep him formal, with few contractions ("I will not", "Let us", "Allow me… May I?").** He is European (the name and the Lithuanian drink suggest it), and the formality is his accent. Fix only the grammar: "made me behave like a human again"; "Let us give each other our hands and let destiny lead us"; "I was this close to nabbing her" (ch 21).
- **Slips out of character** (flag them, and change them only if the author agrees): "Gosh Am I in love?" (ch 23) ("Gosh" is too twee; "God, am I in love?"); "Hell, no." | Character | Policy |
|---|---|
| Benedikt | Keep formal. Don't add contractions. His existing few ("I don't care", "Don't disappoint me") stay. "Let us" stays. |
| Anatoly, Kosovo, Albert | No contractions at all. Remove the one or two they have. |
| Sanchez as Rita / as Benedikt's "assistant" | Formal (it's a mask). Keep as written. |
| Sanchez with Stephen, Maxwell, and in anger or fear | Allow contractions in about 1 of every 2–3 eligible spots, especially negatives: *don't, can't, I'm*. "Let's" is allowed with Stephen. The contrast between her formal mask and her contracted real voice is a cheap, effective device. |
| Stephen | The most contracted voice. "Let's", "I'm", "don't" are all acceptable. Keep his single "Yeah". |
| Maxwell | Formal in the confession. Contract in field orders ("Don't let him go till I come back"). |
| Damian, the thugs, Jules | Contract freely in short lines. |
| Daniel, John, Adriana, Finch | Leave as written, fixing grammar only. |

"Let us" stays for Benedikt, Maxwell's formal moments and the Russians. Change it to "Let's" only in the casual cases: (ch 29), (ch 31), (ch 24), (ch 55), (ch 56), and similar lines between Sanchez and Stephen.

## 6. Rules for the editor: fixing dialogue without making it modern-American or AI-slick

1. **Fix, don't rewrite.** A dialogue edit should usually be deletion (a tag, a tone phrase, a "further") or a mechanical correction (punctuation, a misused verb, a wrong word). Rewrite a line only when it is ungrammatical for that speaker or factually wrong.
2. **Delete before you substitute.** If a tag is wrong, first try removing it. Replace it only if attribution becomes unclear, and replace it with *said* or *asked*.
3. **Keep the author's inventory.** *continued, came the reply, responded, enquired* and an occasional *declared/announced/blurted* stay. The aim is to make them *correct and rarer*, not to remove them.
4. **No new slang, idiom or banter.** Don't add "gonna", "yeah" (beyond Stephen's one), "okay" as a global swap, "you know what?", "seriously", "I mean,", or wisecracks. Don't turn formal lines into snappy fragments.
5. **Contractions only as set out in §4.3.** Nowhere else. In particular, never contract Benedikt's or the Russians' speech.
6. **British and Scottish words for British and Scottish speakers.** Mum, football, *enquire*. This is correcting the characters, not modernising them. Leave American spelling in the narration to the house-style decision; don't mix.
7. **Avoid AI-prose habits.** Don't introduce: "a beat", "voice low", "barely a whisper", "the words hung in the air", "something shifted", "she didn't miss a beat", participial tag chains ("…she said, eyes narrowing, fingers tightening"), rhetorical triplets, or em-dash flourishes in speech. The author's own clichés ("her heart missed many beats") are the author's; trim them, but don't swap in new ones.
8. **Keep each character's sentence length.** Benedikt makes long, courteous speeches. Kosovo is curt. Stephen is breathless. Don't even these out.
9. **Don't "improve" non-native speakers into native speakers.** Jules, Adriana, Albert, Anatoly and Kosovo can keep a slightly formal or foreign English. Correct only what reads as the *author's* error (the pesos, "you guys") rather than the character's.
10. **Long speeches are a structural question, not a copyedit.** For the monologues (ch 50, ch 53, ch 49, (ch 18)), fix the punctuation and grammar in the line edit, and raise conversion to narration as a query to the author. Don't do it silently.
11. **Read each edited exchange aloud with the speaker's policy in mind.** If it sounds like a TV script, back off.
12. **Mechanical cleanup is safe to do globally**, as a separate pass before the line edit: curly quotes, commas inside quotes, "!!" → "!", tag-question commas, vocative commas, "Chief" capitalised, zero-width spaces removed, opening quotes added to multi-paragraph speeches, and "continued further" → "continued".
