# 04 — Description, Imagery & Figurative Language

Scope: I read S01 (ch 0–7), S05 (ch 31–40) and S07 (ch 50–56) in full, and grepped the whole of `book.md` / `paras.json` (≈82k words, 85 chapters). Counts come from regex passes and were then checked by hand. Where a count is approximate, it says so.

---

## 0. Headline findings

1. **Most of the author's figurative language sits in the verbs.** There are only about 70 true similes in 82k words (≈0.85 per 1,000 words), which is modest. Most of the imagery comes from a small set of metaphorical verbs and nouns used over and over: *laced* (22), *aura* (28), *brooding* (23), *demeanor* (21), *sauntered* (17), *graced/gracing* (15), *draped* (10), *engulfed* (10), *repose* (10), *dissolve* (11), and the tic "a **shade of** [emotion]" (≈30). These words carry the voice. They also cause most of the purple effect.
2. **The good images are domestic, small and concrete, and they recur.** The best of them is the daughter wiping frost off the window with her sleeve to watch her father "dissolve in the morning mist" (ch 1). It comes back as the book's emotional keystone: "slowly drifting away into the morning mist, never to return" (ch 50). The bad images are the grand ones: sun, beasts, lightning, "hues of love and shadows of pain."
3. **The author opens with nature and closes on the mind.** Weather or light opens the book and closes it: sun in the haar (ch 1), clouds engulfing the sun (ch 85). The most common chapter ending is a summary of inner state ("her mind was clouded with…").
4. **Women are described by their bodies, men by their clothes and height.** The worst case is the heroine's introduction: a woman "in her late teens" with "full breasts … tantalizing" (ch 2), followed three pages later by an explicit sex scene ch 3. This is the single highest-risk passage in the book for an agent read.
5. **Place description is lavish in interiors and shaky on facts.** Examples: "Stockholm, Denmark" (7 datelines), a 15th-century fado (ch 26), and a Roman palace with pointed arches (ch 3).
6. **Adjective doublets and triplets are frequent.** There are ≥40 emotional-state stacks ("disconcerted, confounded" (ch 2); "worried, perspired and perplexed" (ch 83)) and many redundant pairs ("painful arduous wait" (ch 2), "laborious long search" (ch 50)).

**Governing rule for the edit: cut rather than substitute.** Nearly every problem here goes away by deleting a clause. Almost none needs a new image from us.

## 1. Openings

### 1a. Chapter openings (85 chapters)

Every chapter opens with a **dateline** (time, date, city). The first prose paragraph then follows one of these patterns:

| **Character's emotional state first** ("Benedikt was flabbergasted/furious/devastated") | ≈15 | e.g. ch9 (ch 9), ch28 (ch 28), ch38 (ch 38), ch44 (ch 44), ch59 (ch 59), ch63 (ch 63) |
| Action / logistics / dialogue | rest | ch7 (ch 7), ch34 (ch 34), ch50 (ch 50) … |

Two further patterns:

- **Weather also opens scenes inside chapters.** About 27 paragraphs across the book begin with weather or light in their first two sentences. Examples: "The morning welcomed her with absolute quietude" (ch 54); "The midafternoon sun's coruscating light was seeping through the scattered clouds" (ch 33); "It was a mild afternoon and the sky was spotless" (ch 49); "It was a gorgeous evening" (ch 69).
- **There is a formula, "It was a [adj] [time of day]."** It appears 7×: (ch 12), (ch 49), (ch 69), (ch 72), (ch 76), (ch 78), (ch 85).

**Assessment.** The nature opening is a real habit and an author's signature. The book's frame depends on it: dawn over Girbin (ch 1), then clouds and sun over "the vast valley of wildness" (ch 85). **Keep the habit.** What's wrong with most instances is abstraction and illogic, not the fact that they exist:

- (ch 1) needs the most work. In one paragraph: "the sunbeam was forming a tincture passing through the morning haar"; "The bright dawn sun was camouflaging in the cold wintery brume as if this was the game it had chosen to play"; "the gentle breeze making the chirping fade into some melodious hum"; "it had that uneasiness, the eerie"; "nature's caper". The car is then both "a beast wanting to tame the wilderness of the street" and "blending with the surroundings almost appearing like a time-tested landmark". A beast and a discreet landmark contradict each other. That makes four figurative moves on the book's first page, and the page queried agents will judge.
- (ch 54) "The casts of silver radiance encouraged by the pre-dawn sunrays created a mesmerized stature. And the subtle chilliness dissolved in the air with perfect discretion." These are abstractions built on abstractions.
- (ch 56) "The midmorning sun was fighting hard with its ether to warm the atmosphere with a distinct verve." The image is muddled.

The better openings are plain. "It was pouring in Gydnia. The relentless rain and the gloomy weather were not helping Benedikt to drive away his unwarranted restlessness." (ch 72) does its job in two sentences and ties weather to mood. (ch 83) (mid-chapter) has the best concrete light image in the book: "The summer sun formed a mirage on the long-stressed road and the car appeared as if it was floating on a body of water."

**The waking and "ruffled quietness" openings are over-used.** Eight chapters open on someone waking, and four on a noise breaking silence, which is 12 of 85. Within S05 alone, ch31 opens "The village church bell rang with a loud peal ruffling the quietness of the morning" (ch 31), while ch10 (ch 10) and ch20 (ch 20) use the same verb and noun. Keep the strongest one or two (the church bell (ch 31) is the most specific). For the rest, delete the first sentence and start with the character in motion. Do not invent a new kind of hook.

### 1b. Recommendation for openings

- **Keep one nature beat per chapter opening where the author wrote one, and make it concrete by cutting.** Worked example on (ch 1). Remove the tincture, the camouflage, the "game it had chosen to play", "nature's caper", and "time-tested landmark". What's left is the author's own concrete matter: streetlights giving way to sunshine through the haar, birds, a breeze, people keeping indoors, a black Range Rover rolling to a stop near the town square. The unease survives because the car is still there.
- **Don't let the "It was a ___ morning" formula appear twice within ten chapters.** When two fall close together, cut the weaker one outright.
- **Don't add weather openings where the author didn't write them.**

## 2. Closings

Classification of all 85 chapter endings (last prose paragraph):

| Ending type | ≈Count | Examples |
|---|---|---|
| **Inner-state summary** ("her mind was clouded/crowded…", "with a lost soul and a confused mind") | **~18** | ch5 (ch 5) "her brain froze, and she failed to comprehend anything meaningful"; ch11 (ch 11) "all screaming loud, making a perfect cacophony, with no visible escape path"; ch56 (ch 56) "Her mind was clouded with several questions but sadly she had answers to none"; ch65 (ch 65); ch83 (ch 83) |
| **Character goes to sleep / rests / retires** | **~12** | ch3 (ch 3), ch4 (ch 4), ch9 (ch 9), ch19 (ch 19), ch22 (ch 22), ch28 (ch 28), ch29 (ch 29), ch32 (ch 32), ch47 (ch 47), ch57 (ch 57), ch62 (ch 62) |
| **Cliffhanger / action cut** | **~10** | ch33 (ch 33) "looks like we have some visitors"; ch36 (ch 36) the gun; ch39 (ch 39) "Soon Sanchez went out of her sight"; ch55 ch 55 the photograph; ch58 (ch 58) "she forgot to turn the recorder on"; ch78 (ch 78) "I saw your father" |
| **In transit** ("soon they were on their way…") | **~9** | ch24 (ch 24), ch31 (ch 31), ch38 (ch 38), ch53 (ch 53), ch64 (ch 64), ch74 (ch 74) |
| **Narratorial foreshadowing ("little they knew")** | **2 + 1** | ch1 (ch 1) "little they knew that this Sunday would change their lives forever. Change for the worse."; ch7 (ch 7) "little he knew about the detour she took at Zurich"; mid-chapter (ch 37) "Something they would regret for the rest of their life." |
| **Closing nature image mirroring mood** | **4** | ch26 (ch 26) seagulls by the ocean; ch44 (ch 44) "The evening sun was fading fast… It resembled his state"; ch42 (ch 42); ch85 (ch 85) |

**Assessment.**
- The cliffhanger endings work. They are the thriller's engine, and an editor should not soften them.
- The **"little they knew" hook** is the most dated device in the closings. Agents recognise it at once as author intrusion. It appears only twice, which makes the fix easy. At (ch 1), "Change for the worse" repeats the sentence before it and should be cut. The foreshadowing itself can stay, in the author's words: "…little they knew that this Sunday would change their lives forever." Or end on the image before it, the girl waiting for her father to dissolve in the mist (ch 1), which is stronger. At (ch 7), "Little he knew about the detour she took at Zurich" is functional dramatic irony and can stay. (ch 37) "Something they would regret for the rest of their life" is never paid off, so delete it.
- **The sleep endings and inner-state endings flatten the momentum,** especially when they come in runs (ch28, ch29 end asleep; ch32 ends retiring for the night). Rule: where a chapter ends on a sleep or summary sentence and the paragraph before it ends on a concrete beat, delete the summary so the chapter ends on the concrete beat. Example: ch56 would end on "They started spiraling down the narrow path towards the aft of the ship." [ch 56, first sentence] and lose "sadly she had answers to none."
- **The nature-mirror endings are rare (4) and worth protecting.** (ch 26) is one of the loveliest passages in the book: "The talks receded, and they kept to themselves as if agreeing to an unstated protocol to stay by the ocean for as long as time would permit." (ch 44) states its meaning too plainly ("It resembled his state"). The image can stay if the explanation after the dash is trimmed.

### 3b. Recurring image families

1. **Vapour / mist / dissolving** (the master motif, ≈21 hits). The father dissolves in the mist (ch 1), (ch 50). The bodyguards vanish "like vapor dissolving in air" (ch 2). The tail car "was dissolving at times on the busy streets" (ch 8). Sanchez "dissolved into oblivion" (ch 18). Maxwell takes "the morning mist as his shield" (ch 19). Her guards are "slowly but surely dissolving away" (ch 50); she is "gently dissolving in his aura" (ch 50); "the subtle chilliness dissolved in the air" (ch 54); "dissolved all his reasonings" (ch 78); "dissolved all trepidations" (ch 79). **Verdict:** the literal mist at (ch 1)/(ch 50) is the book's best image. Every casual use of *dissolve* elsewhere weakens it. **Protect (ch 1) and (ch 50). Prune the figurative "dissolve" elsewhere to 2–3 uses,** keeping the ones about disappearance, which echo the father ((ch 8), (ch 18)). Delete (ch 2) "like vapor dissolving in air", which is a bodyguard entering a lift.
2. **Shock as lightning / bolt** (4): "devastated as if a bolt had struck" (ch 44); "pierced Benedikt's heart like a lightning bolt" (ch 72); "like lightning had struck her and bolted her on the chair" (ch 76); "frozen as if lightning had struck him" (ch 85). **Keep one.** (ch 85) sits at the climax, so keep it there and cut the simile clause from the others.
3. **Storm / tempest / weathered** (5): (ch 33), "abate a colossal tempest" (ch 39) (misused: *abate* means lessen; the author means "withstood"), "pale as if he had weathered a storm" (ch 40), "the perfect storm brewing" (ch 59), "weathered the storm" (ch 61). These are all idioms. Trim the doubles.
4. **Predators / beasts** (5): "a beast wanting to tame the wilderness of the street" (ch 1); "guarding the area like a hawk" (ch 25); "like a hawk stooping towards its prey" (ch 59); "like a hawk guarding its prey" (ch 81); "those wolves" (ch 53). The hawk appears three times. Keep (ch 59), which is an action image and accurate ("stooping" is the right falconry verb). Cut the other two.
5. **Royalty / castle / palace** (6): "guarding the passage like a king's castle" (ch 3); "like a Roman palace" (ch 3); "castle-like appearance with gothic structures" (ch 3); "undisputed king" (ch 25); "like a dethroned king, lost and dismayed, aloof and confused" (ch 76); "treat her like a princess" (ch 1). (ch 76) "dethroned king" is earned (Benedikt's fall), but the four adjectives after it bury it.
6. **Shades / hues / tinctures** (≈37, including the "shade of X" tic): "a man of thousand shades, oscillating between hues of love and shadows of pain" (ch 44); "many shades of hues hidden deep within his blue eyes" (ch 42); "his face assumed shades of burned char" (ch 81). Mostly filler. Where "shade of" is a stand-in for "a trace of / a hint of", cut the phrase and keep the emotion.
7. **The mind as a cluttered object**: "his mind had morphed into a confetto, smeared with conflicting thoughts" (ch 7); "his mind was a confetto" (ch 41); "His mind had become a mud puddle, the more he was stirring, the muddier it became" (ch 9); "like a boat without an anchor, drifting to oblivion" (ch 61). *Confetto* is a misuse (a sugared almond; the author probably means *confetti*). It's a pet image, so **query the author** rather than silently change it. **The mud puddle (ch 9) is fresh and homely. Protect it.**
8. **Puzzle / game** (≈20 "puzzle"): "like pieces of a puzzle coming together" (ch 2); "the cat and the mouse game. It was like a puzzle with pieces scattered all over the town… the mystery called Rita" (ch 7); "the entire charade she had put so far, folded like a deck of cards" (ch 76). The card image (ch 76) quietly echoes the poker opening and is worth protecting. (ch 7) has a two-image chain, so keep one.
9. **Shrapnel / physical pain** (2): cold water "pierced like shrapnel" (ch 6); pain "as if he was pricked with shrapnel" (ch 40). Keep one.
10. **Sound personified**: "the walls were humming Mozart's 'The Marriage of Figaro'" (ch 2); "The screeching sound of the tires was dreadful as if providing its final resistance from leaving Sanchez behind" (ch 39); "A dull wind was casually sauntering with a hint of laziness written over it" (ch 45). These are idiosyncratic and often charming. (ch 39) is emotionally right; trim "was dreadful". (ch 45) is the one place *sauntering* earns its keep.

## 4. Character description

### 4a. Introduction habits

- **Men get an inventory of height, suit, brand and face, usually on entrance.** "a visibly tall Caucasian male. He was over six feet tall and was sharply dressed in a cobalt blue Versace suit… his chiseled shape face refused to give any expression away" (ch 2). On the airport tarmac he wears "a dark blue woolen double-breast Armani suit… single knot sky blue tie. An unbuttoned London-Fog trench coat" (ch 21). On the ship: "checkered grey suit… turquoise blue cufflinks… Charcoal colored tie… dark grey boots were shined to perfection" (ch 55). Jules is "a 6 feet tall structure… bulging biceps and chest through the body-hugging t-shirt… endowed Greek like appearance" (ch 29). Anatoly is "a stout well-built man… in the late forties" (ch 78). Tall men are often called a "structure" ((ch 29), (ch 75)), which is an idiosyncrasy worth keeping once.
- **Benedikt's wardrobe is a character device.** "No matter what the situation or occasion was Benedikt never got his sense of dressing wrong" (ch 21). Three full outfit inventories is one too many, but the habit fits him. Keep the first (Versace (ch 2)) and one later one, and trim each to 2–3 items.
- **Women get an inventory of body, hair and allure,** usually through a narrator who endorses the effect ("could make anyone go weak on knees" (ch 2)).
- **Minor characters get one quick physical tag,** which works well: Alberto's "yellow tinted teeth" (ch 2); the cab driver "in his pajamas, looked like he was dragged out of his sleep" (ch 7); Jules's mother "short… stoically built… bright colored bouffant long skirt" (ch 31).

### 4b. Brand and product name-dropping

The count is moderate, about 15 brands book-wide, and most are functional:
- **Fashion:** Versace (ch 2), Armani (ch 21), London-Fog (ch 21), Miss Dior (ch 33).
- **Cars (plot-functional in the chases, keep):** Range Rover (ch 1) / **Land Rover** (ch 1) (a **continuity error**: same car, pick one), BMW 5 series (ch 5), grey Peugeot SUV (ch 5), Audi R8 (ch 19), black Mercedes van (ch 27), "Lamborghinis and Maserati's" (ch 61).
- **Objects:** Mac (ch 31), Aeron chairs and Waterman pen (ch 17), Adele "Set Fire to the Rain" (ch 12), "Forbes list" (ch 17), E. N. Welch clock (spelled "En Welch" (ch 2); fix the spelling, keep the detail).

**Assessment:** the brands aren't excessive and they suit a glamour thriller. The rule is **one brand per character entrance, at most.** At (ch 17) the Aeron chairs, the Waterman pens *and* the eighty-inch television are catalogue copy, so keep one.

For the men, though, the narrator describes clothes and bearing. For the women, the narrator describes breasts, lips and "alluring" effect, and adds approving commentary.

**How a modern publisher or agent would react:** (ch 2) would likely end the read on page 3. The opening pages are what agents read from a query, and "full breasts were tantalizing", applied to the female lead with "late teens" in the same paragraph, is the textbook example of what agents' public wish-lists warn against. Followed by (ch 3), it becomes an age-of-consent concern, not a matter of taste. The later instances ((ch 53), (ch 49), (ch 24)) would be marked as dated "male gaze" and trimmed in a normal line edit rather than rejected outright. None of the women's descriptions need replacing. Each one needs **subtracting** until only the non-body detail is left (gown colour, scarf, hair in a knot, anxious glances at the lobby). (ch 2) without its body clauses still gives a vivid, nervous, overdressed young woman out of place in a hotel café, which is exactly what the scene needs.

## 6. Adjective stacking & redundancy

**Frequency:** a regex for emotion/state-adjective pairs and triplets found **39 stacks in 36 paragraphs**. That is a floor: noun triads and adverb stacks are extra. Roughly one every two chapters, with clusters in emotional scenes (ch50–53, ch76, ch83).

**Types, with examples:**

1. **Synonym doublets and triplets for a feeling**: "She was disconcerted, confounded" (ch 2); "baffled and flustered" (ch 6); "scared and petrified" (ch 15); "startled and shocked" (ch 39); "worried and nervous" (ch 39); "hallucinated and delirious" (ch 50); "dazed and disillusioned" (ch 51); "capricious, temperamental and at times whimsical" (ch 55); "lost and dismayed, aloof and confused" (ch 76); "worried, perspired and perplexed" (ch 83); "perplexed, agitated and scared" (ch 26).
2. **Redundant modifier pairs**: "painful arduous wait" (ch 2); "long arduous wait" (ch 14); "long agonizing wait" (ch 4); "laborious long search" (ch 50); "anxious long wait" (ch 54); "long anxious knocks" (ch 51); "cold wintery brume" (ch 1); "obscured dilapidated house" (ch 32).
3. **Noun triads of abstract states**: "spiraling apprehensions, surging hopes, and fretful uncertainties… the capricious tomorrow" (ch 54); "hunger, filthiness, passion, and desire" (ch 3); "fear, horror, and dreadfulness" (ch 40); "a broken heart, smeared thoughts, and a hope" (ch 83).
4. **Intensifiers**: "utmost" (e.g. "utmost silence" (ch 1), "utmost suave" (ch 37), "utmost discreetness" (ch 74)), "complete", "absolute", "extreme" ("extreme velocity" (ch 2), "extreme caution" (ch 3), (ch 3)).
5. **Tautological description**: "It was black in color" (ch 1); "The room was circular in appearance" (ch 2); "octagonal in shape… also octagonal in form" (ch 17); "The room though had a contemporary appearance, it also sported a modern outlook" (ch 54).

**Rule for trimming:**
- **Emotional doublets and triplets: keep the one most specific adjective the author chose, usually the rarer or more physical one, and delete the rest.** "She was disconcerted, confounded, and was trying hard to conceal it" becomes "She was disconcerted and was trying hard to conceal it." "He was worried, perspired and perplexed" becomes "He was perspiring." (That's the physical detail. Fixing the tense is a grammar edit, not a new word.)
- **Redundant pairs: keep the plainer word.** "painful arduous wait" becomes "arduous wait" (the author likes *arduous*; keep it). "long agonizing wait" becomes "agonizing wait". "laborious long search" becomes "long search".
- **Triads: keep them only where the rhythm is the point** (e.g. (ch 53) "the noise disappeared, the people faded"). Otherwise cut to the strongest member.
- **Intensifiers: delete "utmost / complete / absolute / extreme" unless removing it changes the meaning.**
- **Tautologies: delete the frame and keep the attribute** ("It was black with dark glass shades").
- **Never replace the author's adjective with a synonym of ours.** If none of the author's words works, delete the phrase.

## 7. Rules for the editor: pruning description while keeping the author's sensibility

1. **Delete rather than substitute.** Cut the weaker clause and leave the author's stronger one standing. A sentence we wrote from scratch counts as a failure unless grammar forces it.
2. **Never add a new image, simile or metaphor of our own,** however clever. That includes "better" swaps (Turner for Guggenheim, "fog" for "brume"). If an image is broken, delete it or query the author.
3. **One figurative move per moment.** When a sentence or beat has two or more images (simile + personification + metaphor), keep the most concrete and delete the rest. (ch 1) should end up with at most one.
4. **Keep the nature openings, and make each concrete by subtraction.** Keep the light, weather, sound or place the author named (haar, church bell, rain in Gdynia, mirage on the road). Delete the abstractions around it (*tincture, caper, ether, stature, verve, discretion*). Don't add weather openings where there are none.
5. **Protect the mist motif.** (ch 1) and (ch 50) are sacred. Reduce figurative "dissolve" elsewhere to a handful, and keep the ones that echo disappearance.
6. **Protect the list in §3c.** Those images are the author on a very good day. Trim adjectives around them and never cut the image itself.
7. **Metaphorical-verb tics: thin them, don't ban them.** They are the voice. As rough per-book ceilings after the edit: *laced* ≤8, *graced* ≤6, *engulfed* ≤5, *draped* ≤5, *sauntered* ≤8, *aura* ≤10, "shade of [emotion]" ≤8, "ruffled the quietness" ≤2, "sprang to action" ≤3. When over the ceiling, cut the whole phrase rather than swapping in a synonym ("A sign of relief graced his visage" becomes "He was relieved", using the author's own adjective from context where possible).
8. **Repeated similes: one per book.** Lightning struck (4) → 1 (ch 85). Hawk (3) → 1 (ch 59). Shrapnel (2) → 1. "like a charm" (2) → 1. "time-tested" (4) → 0–1 (misused).
9. **Character introductions:** at most one brand and at most three physical or clothing details per entrance. Keep height and one garment for men. For women, **remove body-part inventory (breasts, bosom, "well-toned body", cleavage, "full lips" as the lead detail) and keep dress, hair, gesture and behaviour.** Delete narratorial approval ("could make anyone go weak on knees", "the epitome of bravery").
10. **Sex scenes:** don't rewrite them into something else. Delete the most explicit clauses ("filthiness", "ogled… Long enough to arouse her", "exploring her body inch by inch") and let the author's own softer sentences carry the moment ("They made love again and again like there was no tomorrow" can stay). **Nothing goes out until the story team has fixed the heroine's age so she is unambiguously adult.**
11. **Interiors: one sentence of inventory plus one telling detail.** Delete directional furniture lists ("On the far-right side…") unless the layout matters to the action (the poker table seating (ch 2) does; the warehouse sofa (ch 42) doesn't).
12. **Place facts: one per location, and only if it touches character or plot.** Route every factual error in §5c to fact-check. Don't silently "correct" a place into one the author didn't choose.
13. **Adjective stacks: keep one, preferring the physical or specific one the author used.** Cut redundant pairs to the plainer word. Delete intensifiers.
14. **Chapter endings:** keep cliffhangers untouched. Where a chapter ends in a sleep or inner-state summary, cut the summary if the beat before it is concrete. Remove "little they knew" only where it adds nothing ((ch 1)'s "Change for the worse." and (ch 37)).
15. **Leave the register alone.** The formal, slightly antique diction (*repose, visage, sauntered, orotund*) is the author. We thin its frequency. We don't modernise it into plain contemporary prose, and we don't replace it with a different ornate register of our own.
16. **Misused words inside images** (*tincture, confetto, occult, abate, stature, time-tested*): delete the image if it can go. If it's load-bearing, flag it to the author with a one-line query rather than choosing the word ourselves.
