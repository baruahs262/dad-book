## 1. Narrative stance

### 1.1 Omniscient versus close third

The book **aims at rotating close third and slides into omniscience**. Each chapter opens clearly anchored in one character: "Benedikt was flabbergasted." (ch 9); "Maxwell deplaned with extreme urgency." (ch 25); "Francis was enraged." (ch 28). Within the chapter, though, the narrator treats every mind as open. This is a stable feature of the voice. It is not a slip made once or twice. The author thinks of the narrator as a storyteller standing above the action, who can tell you what the passers-by thought ((ch 24) "To passersby's it appeared as if two lovers were meeting after a long time") or what a bus-stand tout suspected ((ch 24) "The boy sensed that he might have gotten a customer").

The book also has real **omniscient narration that the viewpoint character could not know**:
- (ch 5) "Despite her alertness, she missed noticing the shadow that was following her from Denmark." This is written from inside her chapter, but the narrator knows what she does not.
- (ch 7) "While he assumed she was in Basel, little he knew about the detour she took at Zurich."
- (ch 13) "Though they both held an impassive posture, she could clearly see through them on how impressed they were." Here the narrator's knowledge is laundered through her.

Neither is overused yet, and both are part of the voice. **Keep them, but do not let them multiply.** ch 47 is the weakest: there the formula stands in for motivation the scene should supply. That goes in the editorial letter, not the line edit.

### 1.3 Travel-guide and encyclopaedic asides (narrator as host)

This is a distinctive authorial intrusion: the narrator stops to explain the culture.
- (ch 8) Sechseläuten and the Böögg effigy.
- (ch 24) "The seven skirts represented the digit seven which had ethnic significance in Portuguese culture – mostly calling out the seven days, seven colors of the rainbow or the biblical seven waves."
- (ch 24) Lisbon City Hall, "rebuilt in the late 18th century".
- (ch 26) Fado: "Fado express sadness, but the version performed by the band hardly reflected any glumness."
- (ch 49) "Sunny days were short-lived in this part of the world…"
- (ch 25) "since Lisbon was the main port of entry to Portugal most of the travelers initiated and concluded their journey here."

These are part of the book's pleasure and part of the voice: an author who loves places and wants to show you round. At line level they can be trimmed and corrected, but not removed wholesale. Whether to cut some of them for pace is an editorial-letter decision.

### 1.4 Head-hopping: the worst instances

Grades: **Severe** means three or more minds in one scene, or a mid-paragraph switch that blurs who is feeling. **Moderate** means one intrusive dip into a minor character.

1. **ch 47 ch 47, the jet (Severe).** Benedikt (ch 47) → Sanchez (ch 47) → the *doctor's* mind (ch 47) ("He had no answer for her. He himself was in captivation and unsure where he was headed") → Benedikt (ch 47) → Sanchez (ch 47), and in the same paragraph Benedikt ("Benedikt saw how her face turned pale… He felt terrible seeing her in such a helpless state") → Sanchez (ch 47) → Benedikt (ch 47) ("By now he knew stubbornness came to her innately") → Sanchez ch 47. This is the most important head-hop in the book, because the scene depends on the reader not knowing whether Sanchez's softening is real.
2. **ch 48 ch 48, phone call to HQ (Severe).** Maxwell's scene enters the switchboard lady ("She knew Maxwell well… She knew Maxwell meant business today" [ch 48]), then Drake ("he tolerated him because he was the best he had" (ch 48); "made Drake furious" (ch 48); "Drake hardly had the energy left to argue" (ch 48)), and returns to Maxwell [ch 48]. That is three minds in eight paragraphs.
3. **ch 43 ch 43, interrogation (Severe).** The scene is Maxwell's until (ch 43): "William was lost. He wasn't sure where his friends were… From the conversation, he concluded that his friends got the girl." The torture beat then loses its viewpoint.
4. **ch 49 ch 49, Tallinn breakfast and lunch (Moderate to Severe).** Paragraphs alternate between Benedikt ((ch 49), (ch 49), (ch 49), (ch 49)) and Sanchez ((ch 49), (ch 49), (ch 49), (ch 49), (ch 49)). The alternation *is* the romance technique, so it may be kept as a deliberate "dual close" chapter. However, (ch 49)–(ch 49) switch mid-exchange, and (ch 49) and (ch 49) swing from her guilt to his gaze with no break.
5. **(ch 27) ch 27 (Severe, inside one paragraph).** Maxwell is watching Francis from a café table, yet the narration reports Francis's interior: "After some time of scurrying around, he realized his worst fear was a reality now." Maxwell cannot know that.
6. **(ch 41) ch 41 (Moderate, but with a naming problem).** In a Stephen scene, the narration enters "the man" (Maxwell): "He couldn't believe Stephen left her in the hands of the goons." The same chapter keeps Maxwell anonymous ("a young man" (ch 41), "the stranger" (ch 41)) because Stephen doesn't know him, even though the reader has known Maxwell since ch 19. The anonymity and the mind-reading contradict each other.
7. **ch 8 ch 8, the siblings in the car (Moderate).** Sanchez [ch 8 → Stephen [ch 8, end of paragraph: "He kept a close eye on the rear-view mirror… thinking of ways to bolt away"] → Stephen ch 8 → Sanchez [ch 8] → Stephen ch 8 ("Stephen could read her mind…"). This is a tidy paragraph-by-paragraph alternation. It is mild, and typical of the book's two-hander scenes.
8. **(ch 24) and (ch 24) ch 24 (switch inside a paragraph).** (ch 24) opens with Sanchez at the window and then: "Although Stephen was awake he kept his eyes closed, trying to get whatever respite he could…". (ch 24) opens with "Stephen lost the battle… disgruntled and hungry" and then: "Leaving Lisbon was the utmost priority for her, and while she knew Stephen was famished she put a deaf ear to his request."
9. **ch 2 ch 2 (Moderate).** The scene starts from the girl in the café, whose "anxious but wouldn't show" (ch 2) is her interior. At ch 2 it moves into Benedikt: "There was something in her that was noble, which urged him to know more about her." Since the ch 2 opening deliberately uses the camera eye (a man in a Versace suit, a petite girl), this hop could be justified as omniscient, but it should be consistent.
10. **(ch 13) ch 13 (Minor).** In Sanchez's interview, the narration moves into David: "that was the prime intent behind the question, as he wanted her to respond impromptu."
11. **Walk-on minds.** The boy (ch 24); the armed immigration guard (ch 25) ("She saw a person running with urgency towards her"); the receptionist (ch 45) ("The girl knew she was giving away information that she wasn't allowed by the protocol"). These are harmless and very characteristic. A line editor can soften them to observable behaviour ("she hesitated, then…").
12. **ch 42 ch 42.** Marty's gang is shown externally, then Benedikt's pounding heart (ch 42) and yearning ch 42 take over. This is acceptable as a chapter-internal handover, but the chapter has no stated anchor.

**Pattern:** the hops happen in **two-handers** (siblings, Benedikt/Sanchez, Maxwell/anyone) and in **action scenes** (the narrator wants every reaction). Monologue chapters (Benedikt ch 9; Maxwell ch 25/30; Francis ch 28) are nearly clean.

## 2. Showing versus telling

### 2.1 The default method is to name the emotion, then give a physical tag

The author's normal way of conveying emotion is: **[Name] was [emotion adjective(s)].** It is often stacked, and often followed by a stock bodily correlate or a tone-of-voice tag.

- Stacked labels: (ch 2) "She was disconcerted, confounded, and was trying hard to conceal it"; (ch 26) "He was perplexed, agitated and scared, all at the same time."; (ch 49) "making him thrilled, nervous, dizzy, all at once."
- **Chapter-opening labels** (a recurring device): (ch 9) "Benedikt was flabbergasted."; (ch 28) "Francis was enraged."; (ch 44) "Benedikt was devastated as if a bolt had struck shattering him to pieces."; (ch 59) "Benedikt was furious."; (ch 63) "Benedikt's aggravation was at the extreme."
- Face as a screen: (ch 15) "A thousand emotions were crossing his face and from being confused he soon transformed into a scared person."; (ch 42) "A thousand emotions crossed his face."
- Abstract "sense of" nouns: (ch 8) "a sign of relief graced his visage"; (ch 49) "Suddenly a wave of sadness covered her soul… A wave of guilt swept over her soul"; (ch 44) "a sense of calmness was slowly engulfing his soul".
- Voice as the carrier of emotion: (ch 9) "the voice had deep frail of vagueness"; (ch 25) "his taut voice couldn't hide his infuriation"; (ch 41) "his voice trailed laced with fear and helplessness".

Note that *delirious* (14 uses) nearly always means "agitated or excited", not "confused by illness". The editor should correct it where it misleads ((ch 27), (ch 43), (ch 47)) and keep it where it only colours the tone. That is the vocabulary report's call.

### 2.3 Where the author does show, and does it well

Showing mostly appears in **physical comedy and gesture**, and these moments are the book's best:
- (ch 24) "Sanchez rolled her eyes, she stopped walking and her hands naturally came and rested on her hip"
- (ch 8) "She dropped her shoulders as if she had heaved away a long-carried burden, passed a frail smile to Stephen, and closed her eyes."
- ch 26 Stephen's panic and Sanchez's calm "Stephen, come sit beside me." The contrast carries the emotion.
- (ch 28) "Francis' throat went dry and for some miserable long seconds, he failed to speak."
- (ch 49) "He cursed himself as he fumbled to switch it off."
- (ch 30) Maxwell finding his own keys left hanging in the van door.

**Line-edit rule:** do not convert labels into gestures wholesale. That would be an AI rewrite of the voice. Instead: (a) where a label is followed by a good gesture, cut the label and let the gesture work (e.g. (ch 8) "Sanchez perturbations were testing her patience. From time to time she craned her neck sideways…"); (b) collapse stacked triples to one precise word; (c) fix misapplied labels (*delirious*, *disheveled* for a voice (ch 15), *stature* for posture). Keep chapter-opening labels. They are a signature.

## 3. Interiority: how thoughts are rendered

| Mode | Frequency | Examples |
|---|---|---|
| Psycho-narration ("He knew / She was aware / was certain / decided") | Dominant: "He/She knew" ×139, "was certain/sure/convinced" ×36, "decided" ×72 | (ch 8) "He knew what Sanchez had accomplished was the first small step…"; (ch 47) "One thing was evident – Benedikt had a soft corner for her." |
| Free indirect questions (in narration, unquoted) | 16 in 13 paragraphs | (ch 8) "Could this be a miscreant of Benedikt's horde? Or was it someone that she wasn't aware of?"; (ch 6) "How could a girl make such a big mockery of him?"; (ch 28); (ch 53) |
| "How he/she wished…" exclamative | 8 | (ch 28) "How he wished he could get hold of her before Benedikt's arrival."; (ch 37), (ch 50), (ch 76) |
| Quoted thought + "he thought" | about 3 | (ch 30) ""Perhaps Benedikt was made aware of Sanchez disappearance", he thought"; (ch 46) "How would she react… Will she accept my love?" (quoted, untagged); (ch 75) ""He surely had picked his English from dad." She thought." |
| Thought voiced aloud: "mumbled" / "spoke to himself" | 13 + 3 | (ch 9) "mumbled, "It has to be that map.""; (ch 9) ""No one can corrupt it…", he spoke to himself"; ch 25 Maxwell's "How is this possible?… he mumbled"; (ch 41) Stephen "How can I leave her alone?" he mumbled. |
| Italics | **0** | none |

**Consistency:** the mix is fairly stable, and the author's main alternative to italic thought is to have the character **mutter it aloud**. That fits the voice (it is the stage-play instinct that also produces all the dialogue tags). The inconsistencies:
- Quoted thoughts appear in some places with "he thought" (ch 30), in others untagged (ch 46, ch 52: "Did she dodge Benedikt?… Maxwell's mind was spinning"), and in others with a capitalised tag after a full stop (ch 75).
- The punctuation varies: comma outside the quote ((ch 9), (ch 30)), a stray full stop plus a capital ((ch 75)).

**Recommendation (line level, house-style):** do not introduce italics as a new device throughout the book. Standardise quoted thought to one pattern: `"…," he thought.`, the pattern the author already uses most. Leave the "mumbled" device alone; it is characteristic. Where a free-indirect question is followed by an explanatory sentence that repeats it, trim the repeat.

## 4. Exposition, backstory and recaps

### 4.1 Architecture

The backstory is delivered in **three big blocks**:
1. **Ch 1 (2010)**: a prologue in the omniscient storyteller's mode, where the daughter is not named ("his daughter"), ending "Change for the worse." (ch 1).
2. **Part II (ch 11–18, 2012, Ayr)**: a full dramatised flashback, told in continuous close third on Sanchez.
3. **Ch 50**: Sanchez tells her story aloud to Benedikt over lunch in Tallinn (about 2,470 words, mostly her direct speech, with bullet-list notes ch 50).

### 4.2 Recaps of material the reader has already seen

- **Ch 50 retells ch 1.** (ch 50) "I saw him for the last time from the windowsill of my room, slowly drifting away into the morning mist" echoes (ch 1) almost image for image. It then adds genuinely new material (the police officer MacCallan (ch 50), the notebook in the basement ch 50) and **summarises** Part II in one paragraph instead of retelling it: (ch 50) "She told him how she started working for TransPacific, her quest in finding John Paterson and her interaction with Bradley." That summary is a good instinct, and the model for any trims.
- **Parallel chapters retell one event from two sides.** The Montijo jet arrival is dramatised in Francis's POV ch 28 and then re-watched by Maxwell in ch 30 ("He saw them breaking into a conversation and soon Benedikt was throwing his hands in the air"). The Lisbon airport escape is shown from Sanchez ch 24, from Maxwell ch 25 and from Francis ch 28. This is the book's structure, not a slip, but each retelling repeats beats the reader already has.
- **In-scene "replays."** Characters routinely rerun the plot in their heads: (ch 8) "She replayed in her mind everything that happened during her final dash from the hotel"; (ch 8); (ch 9) "He went back to the drawing board and replayed the events that had unfolded so far"; (ch 30) "While waiting Maxwell played the whole event back on his mind". These are useful for a reader, but they are the main source of drag.
- **Explanations in dialogue.** ch 49 Benedikt explains how he traced her (much of it new to the reader, so keep it). ch 9 Benedikt briefs Damian on the station reasoning we just watched him do ch 9. (ch 25) and (ch 25) Maxwell twice gives the officers the same plea.
- **Recap by summary sentence**, e.g. (ch 43) "told the entire episode in an embarrassing tone". This is good compression and should be encouraged.

**Line level:** trim the replays to one sentence where the reader saw the event within the previous two chapters (e.g. (ch 8)'s first half, (ch 30)'s first sentences). Keep the ch 50 monologue at line level. Its length and any cut of the ch 1 duplication belong in the **editorial letter**.

## 5. Character naming in narration

### 5.1 Rita / Sanchez (the cover name)

- **Rita is a cover name.** She introduces herself to Benedikt as Rita ((ch 2) "My name is Rita"; (ch 2)). The narration then **adopts the cover name** for ch 2: "Rita noted a slight change in Alberto's stance" (ch 2), (ch 2), (ch 2), (ch 2), (ch 2), (ch 2), (ch 2). It uses it again in Benedikt's investigation, ch 6–7 ((ch 6), (ch 7), (ch 7), (ch 7), (ch 7), (ch 7)). "Rita" occurs 23 times, all in ch 2–7.
- **In her own action chapters** (ch 3 escape, ch 5 train) the narration avoids naming her at all: "she" only (ch 3, ch 5). This is careful and deliberate.
- **The reveal** is made through Damian at (ch 7): "Her name isn't Rita. At least her photo ID suggests Sanchez." (ch 7) frames it as "the real name of the girl". From ch 8 (ch 8) onward the narration uses "Sanchez" (490 uses) and never "Rita" again.
- **Verdict:** this is consistent and effective. **Do not normalise Rita to Sanchez in ch 2–7.** The only wobble is that ch 2's narration calls her "Rita" in her *own* interior beats, which slightly contradicts the pronoun-only strategy of ch 3 and 5. That is a question for the editorial letter, and it is defensible either way.
- **Her full name.** She is *Sanchez Roberto*: her letter is signed "Sanchez Roberto." (ch 13); the family is "the Roberto family" (ch 1), father Daniel Roberto (ch 1), and Maxwell asks for "Sanchez and Stephen Roberto" (ch 25). Her pet name is **"San"** (Stephen (ch 10), her father (ch 50)). **Inconsistency to flag:** the TransPacific letter addresses her as "Dear Ms. Sanchez" (ch 13), treating the given name as a surname. That could be an in-world clerical slip, but it will read as an author error. Query the author.

### 5.2 The agent: tracksuit man / Trevor / Bradley / Maxwell

One character has four labels, and **the narration always uses whatever name the viewpoint character knows him by**:
- Ch 4 ch 4: "A man dressed in a black tracksuit", unnamed.
- Ch 7–9, 20: "Trevor", used only by Benedikt and Damian ((ch 7), (ch 9) "Trevor wasn't his real name", (ch 9), (ch 20)). The narration never calls him Trevor in its own voice outside Benedikt's POV.
- Part II (ch 17–18): "Bradley Faucet" (ch 17), her manager in Sanchez's POV.
- From ch 19 onward, in his own POV: "Maxwell" (158 uses).
- The reunion in ch 53 ties them together: in Sanchez's POV "Sanchez was extremely uncomfortable seeing Bradley" (ch 53); Stephen says "his real name is Maxwell" (ch 53); she says "Bradley, Maxwell or whosoever you are" (ch 53).

**Verdict:** this is sophisticated and consistent. Preserve it. Two cautions:
- (a) (ch 53) "He was always a mystery to him" should be "to her". That is a line fix.
- (b) Ch 41 ch 41 keeps Maxwell as "a young man / the stranger / the man" in Stephen's POV (correct by the same rule, since Stephen hasn't met him), but then enters Maxwell's mind (ch 41). The line fix is to make (ch 41) observable ("His face said he couldn't believe…"), or to leave it for the letter.

### 5.3 Other names and epithets

- **"The girl" (28 in narration, 11 in dialogue)** is almost exclusively the villains' word, used in Benedikt, Damian, Francis, Marty and William scenes ((ch 6), ch 6, (ch 28), (ch 28), (ch 28) "this girl was no ordinary", (ch 37) ×5, (ch 42), (ch 43)). In the Roberto chapters she is "Sanchez" or "she". This is **POV-coherent and should be preserved**. Other variants: "the uncanny girl" (ch 49), "his limping lady" (ch 49) (Benedikt's fond epithet, and a nice touch), "the injured girl" (ch 46).
- **Stephen's introduction by delay**: "the person behind the wheels" (ch 5) → "her companion" (ch 8) → "Stephen" (ch 8) → "her brother" (ch 8). The sibling relationship is revealed obliquely, and it works. Keep it.
- **Minor characters stay anonymous by role**: "the lady" (receptionists and switchboard, 17), "the chief" (the immigration chief, and Benedikt as "chief" to his men), "his police officer friend" (never named, even at his death ch 43). This is characteristic, but the dead officer's anonymity blunts (ch 43) ("Against his wish, Maxwell ignored his friend"). That is for the editorial letter: name him once.
- **David / Dave:** he is "David Sussex" (ch 13) and "David" ch 13, ch 14], then the narration switches to "Dave" (ch 15), (ch 17) with no signal. This is a line-level consistency fix; recommend "David" in narration.
- **Patterson / Paterson:** "John Paterson" (ch 50) against "Patterson" everywhere else. Fix.
- **Benedkit** (ch 78) is a typo.
- **Name frequency:** the narration names characters very often instead of using pronouns. "Benedikt" occurs 513 times and "Sanchez" 490, and full names recur in consecutive sentences ((ch 10) "Sanchez was still sleeping… it was time for Sanchez to wake up"; ch 24 "Sanchez" ×4). Pronoun substitution where there is no ambiguity is a legitimate line edit.

## 6. Humour, warmth and moral tone

**Humour** is gentle, situational and affectionate, never cynical:
- Stephen's hunger as a running gag through ch 24–26: (ch 24) "His desperation to have a good meal was beyond comprehension"; (ch 24) "What if we go to the waterfront?"; (ch 24) "with dropped shoulders followed Sanchez, disgruntled and hungry"; the payoff (ch 26) "If it wasn't for you we would have been hungry and tortured." and (ch 26) "Sanchez couldn't help from having a good laugh".
- Jules's clowning: (ch 29) "Irmã that is because of Stephen's influence. When you meet, smart people like me ideas start to flow"; (ch 29) the roaming excuse, "They all grinned."
- Schadenfreude at the villains' expense: (ch 25) Maxwell wants to see "Francis's expression"; (ch 27) "Maxwell couldn't resist from smiling and enjoyed every moment of Francis's despondency"; (ch 44) "He finally got something right even if it was by accident."; (ch 28) Benedikt's "you useless baboons".
- Self-deprecating slapstick: (ch 30) Maxwell's keys "hanging from the van door key knob… 'How could I do this?'"
- A light flirtation beat: ch 45 the receptionist's dinner invitation and Maxwell's "Maybe."
- The father–daughter muffin ritual ch 1, "for reasons best known to them".

**Warmth:** the emotional centre is family. There are the father's Sundays (ch 1), sibling tenderness ((ch 8) "He looked at her and saw a sense of tranquility covering her stature. He smiled and got back to business."; (ch 26) the two on the bench by the sea), Aunt Naiena (ch 11), and Jules's household ("Mãe must be asleep" (ch 29)). The cover letter ch 13, with the reindeer, the postman and "My father was a delivery man", is a good example of the author's sincerity. Even the villain is given longing and gentleness ((ch 42), (ch 49) "When you are around, there is calmness. There is serenity.").

**Moral tone:** the moral framework is clear and old-fashioned. Villains are "goons" (11), "ruffians" and "miscreants" (22 combined). The heroine's deceit troubles her conscience ((ch 49) "She hated the thought of messing with his emotions… A wave of guilt swept over her soul"). The narrator praises decency even in henchmen ((ch 37) "He refrained from taking any advantage of the situation and avoided touching her with any shade of lust"). Violence by the good side is presented as justified resolve ((ch 43) "Maxwell meant every word. He fired a shot right on his leg."). Institutions are suspect: police who turned cold (ch 15), (ch 50), local officials who "would support Benedikt without any reasoning" (ch 8). Female description tends to the admiring and romantic ((ch 2), (ch 10), (ch 49), (ch 53)). This is a voice trait. The editor may lightly de-objectify phrasing (for example, (ch 2) "her full breasts were tantalizing" in a first-glance description) but should not remove the romantic gaze.

## 7. The voice fingerprint: what an editor must preserve

1. **The chapter dateline and the rotating lead character.** "4:30 PM 17th April 2014, Lisbon, Portugal" and one principal POV per chapter, with parallel overlapping timelines ((ch 24), (ch 25), (ch 27), (ch 28), (ch 30)). This is the book's skeleton and its "thriller" signal.
2. **The storyteller's hook-lines at thresholds.** "little they knew that this Sunday would change their lives forever. Change for the worse." (ch 1); "little he knew about the detour she took at Zurich" (ch 7). Keep them, and keep them rare.
3. **"For reasons best known to her / them."** (ch 1), (ch 13). The narrator's affectionate wink. Keep, and do not add more.
4. **The name-plus-emotion chapter opener.** "Benedikt was flabbergasted." (ch 9); "Francis was enraged." (ch 28). Keep the device; line edits may choose a truer adjective.
5. **The formal, slightly ceremonious register** in both narration and dialogue: "Let us get back to our apartment" (ch 8), "Let us take a walk instead" (ch 15), with no "let's" anywhere. Characters give full, well-formed speeches ((ch 13), ch 9, (ch 49)). Do not casualise dialogue into contemporary American snap.
6. **Latinate, "big-word" diction as colour**: *repose, visage, countenance, perturbation, quandary, trepidation, sauntered, solicitude*. Correct misuses, but keep the texture. Replace a wrong exotic word with a *right* exotic word where possible, not with a plain one (e.g. "descry" (ch 8) is correct, so keep it).
7. **Thinking aloud.** Characters "mumble" their thoughts: (ch 9), ch 25, (ch 28), (ch 41). There are no italics, so do not introduce them.
8. **Free-indirect rhetorical questions in clusters of two or three** at moments of doubt: (ch 8), (ch 15), (ch 28), (ch 52).
9. **"How he/she wished…"** (ch 28), (ch 50), (ch 76). It is a small device but a recurring one.
10. **The homely metaphor or aphorism dropped into narration**: "His mind had become a mud puddle, the more he was stirring, the muddier it became." (ch 9); "the things you run away from haunt you back more fiercely" (ch 10).
11. **The host's love of place**: brief cultural and culinary asides (Fado (ch 26), the seven skirts (ch 24), Douro red and cod in olive oil (ch 26), Tallinn's Freedom Square (ch 49)). Trim them for accuracy and length, but do not delete their presence.
12. **POV-filtered naming**: Rita in ch 2–7; Trevor in Benedikt's mind; Bradley in Sanchez's; Maxwell in his own; "the girl" in the villains' chapters. Preserve it exactly.
13. **Gentle comedy of appetite, clumsiness and villain comeuppance** (Stephen famished; Maxwell's keys; Francis "got something right even if it was by accident"). Protect the comic beats from being cut for pace.
14. **Sincere, unironic emotion and moral clarity.** Characters feel big, say so, and mean it ((ch 49), (ch 49)). An editor must not add irony or modern knowingness.
15. **The descriptive "sweep" before action**: weather, sun and breeze to open a scene ((ch 10) "The alarm clock ruffled the quietness of the otherwise serene morning"; (ch 24), (ch 45) "A dull wind was casually sauntering with a hint of laziness written over it"). Tighten these, but keep the establishing beat.

## 8. What may change at line level without violating the voice

These are safe line edits that read like "the author on a good day":
- **Grammar, idiom and article repair** that leaves the formality in place: "few" → "a few" (187 bare "few", most need "a"), "believe" → "belief" ((ch 9), (ch 9)), "disbelieve" → "disbelief", "Sanchez perturbations" → "Sanchez's", "went pass" → "went past", "runaway" → "runway" (ch 46).
- **Misapplied vocabulary** swapped for a correct word of similar register: "delirious" when "agitated" is meant, "stature" when "posture/composure" is meant ((ch 10) "regained her lost stature"), "disheveled voice" (ch 15), "confetto" (ch 41), "absolvent" (ch 43), "insentient soul" (ch 43), "metaphor to a suffocating zone" (ch 13), "rue of sarcasm" (ch 43).
- **Collapse stacked emotion labels** to the single best word, and cut a label when a good gesture follows it (see §2.3).
- **Dialogue tags.** Reduce "blurted" (56) to real blurting; convert some *stated / announced / declared / queried* to "said" or "asked", or drop them where the speaker is clear. Do **not** strip them all: tag variety is part of the voice. Aim to roughly halve the non-"said" tags in heavy scenes and remove "with a ___ tone" where the dialogue already carries the tone.
- **Pronoun for repeated proper names** where there is no ambiguity.
- **Small head-hop repairs inside a paragraph:** turn a minor character's interior into observable behaviour ((ch 24) boy, (ch 25) guard, (ch 45) receptionist, (ch 13) David, (ch 27) Francis's "realized" → something Maxwell can see). Also split paragraphs that switch mind mid-paragraph ((ch 24), (ch 24), (ch 47)).
- **Trim in-scene replays** to one sentence when the event is within the last two chapters ((ch 8), (ch 9), (ch 30)).
- **Standardise thought punctuation** to `"…," he thought.`
- **Name consistency:** David/Dave → David; Paterson → Patterson; Benedkit → Benedikt; (ch 53) "to him" → "to her".
- **Travel-guide asides:** correct the facts and cut them to one or two sentences each.

## 9. Out of scope for line editing (for the editorial letter)

- **The POV regime itself.** Whether to commit to strict one-POV-per-scene close third or to own the omniscient narrator openly. Key test scenes: ch 47 jet ch 47, ch 48 phone ch 48, ch 49 Tallinn ch 49, ch 43 interrogation ch 43. The recommendation is to *own* the omniscience with a consistent narrator presence, since the voice already has one, and to fix only the scenes where the head-hopping destroys suspense (ch 47: is Sanchez's softening real?).
- **The overlapping parallel chapters** (ch 24–30 retell the airport and Montijo from three sides). Consider merging or cutting duplicate beats. This is structural.
- **The ch 50 monologue** (about 2,500 words, retelling ch 1 and summarising Part II). Consider cutting its ch 1 duplication and keeping only the new material (MacCallan, the notebook, the basement).
