# House Style Sheet and Self-Edit Checklist

These are the conventions the edit applied. Use them for any new pages you write during revision, so the new material matches the edited book.

---

## Part A. Conventions

### Spelling
- **British spelling, with -ise endings:** colour, favourite, realise, recognise, centre, metre, grey, travelled, jewellery, pyjamas, tyre, sceptical, programme (for a TV or exchange programme). This is applied in the tracked manuscript as a separate set of changes under "Editor (Spelling)". If you decide to submit to American publishers instead, reject that whole set.
- Keep your existing British habits: *towards*, *amongst*, *enquire/enquired*.
- **Scottish characters say** *Mum*, *football*, *trousers*, *flat*, *pavement*.
- **Place names:** Helsingør, Zurich, St. Gallen-Altenrhein, Lisbon, Nazaré, O Sítio, Tallinn, Paljassaare, Ventspils, Gdynia, Warsaw, Vitebsk, Liozna, Smolensk, Girbin (fictional), Ayr.

### Numbers, money and time
- **Numbers in running text are words:** "a few hundred metres", "thirty minutes", "two million pounds".
- **Currencies are lower case:** pounds, euros, kroner, roubles.
- **Chapter date lines** keep your format: `4:30 AM 16th April 2014, Zurich, Switzerland`. Use local time.
- **In the text,** write times in words ("half past eight"), or "8:30" when a character reads a clock.

### Punctuation
- **Dashes.** Use a spaced en dash ( – ), which is your own habit. Use it sparingly: at most one in a paragraph. Never use the long em dash (—) or a hyphen as a dash.
- **Semicolons and colons.** Avoid them in narration. You use very few, and that is part of your voice. Mend a run-on sentence with a full stop or "and".
- **Exclamation marks.** Never double (!!). Use them rarely in narration.
- **Question marks.** Only on real questions. "Tell me more about yourself." ends with a full stop.
- **Quotation marks.** Use curly double quotes for speech (“ ”), and single quotes for a quote inside a quote.

### Dialogue punctuation
| Pattern | Example |
|---|---|
| Speech, then tag | “Drive as fast as you can,” he told Damian. |
| Tag, then speech | He said, “Why don’t you leave me alone?” |
| Question or exclamation, then tag | “Can you hear the footsteps?” she asked. |
| Speech, then action (no speech verb) | “Try me once.” There was excitement in his voice. |
| Split sentence | “Sanchez,” Stephen said, “he isn’t a bad person.” |
| Tag question | “Leave me alone, will you?” |
| Name in address | “Sorry, Chief. The flight has left.” |
| Speech running over several paragraphs | Open quotes at the start of every paragraph; close quotes only at the end of the last. |

## Part B. Your habits to watch when writing new pages

Your voice is formal, earnest, courteous and descriptive. Keep it. The habits below are the ones that most often made a sentence go wrong in the first draft. Search for them in any new chapter before you consider it finished.

1. **Words that sound right but mean something else.** These were the most frequent problem.
   | Word | What it means | What you wanted |
   |---|---|---|
   | *stature* | height or reputation | posture, composure, bearing |
   | *delirious* | out of one's mind with fever | frantic, agitated |
   | *outlook* | a view of the future | expression, look |
   | *orotund* | pompous | loud, booming |
   | *accost* | to approach someone aggressively | approach, speak to |
   | *discrete* | separate | discreet (careful) |
   | *inhabitations* | homes | inhibitions |
   | *captivated* | charmed | captured |
   | *disbelieve* | a verb | disbelief (the noun) |

   When you reach for an unusual word, check the dictionary definition, not just the thesaurus.
2. **"A few" versus "few".** "A few feet away" means some. "Few people came" means hardly any. Most of the time you mean *a few*.
3. **The possessive 's.** Write "Benedikt's turn", "Sanchez's voice", "the waitress's eyes". Don't drop it.
4. **Past tense throughout.** Watch for slips into the present ("He puts the spread on the table" should be "He put…"). After "until", "once" or "if", don't use *would* ("until one of them was declared the winner", not "would be declared").
5. **Speech tags.** *Said* and *asked* are invisible, and that is their virtue. Use *blurted*, *announced*, *stated* and *declared* only when the speaker really does blurt or announce. Never *hushed*, *blazoned* or *spoke* before a quotation.
6. **One colour per line of dialogue.** Choose a strong verb, *or* a tone ("in a low voice"), *or* an action. Never all three. "With a possessive voice he queried, 'May I?'" becomes "He looked at her. 'May I?'"
7. **Naming emotions in pairs.** "She was disconcerted, confounded" becomes "She was shaken". Pick the one best word.
8. **Retelling.** If the reader has already seen a scene, don't have a character recount it at length. One sentence of summary is enough.
9. **One head per scene.** Stay inside one character's thoughts until the scene breaks. If you need another point of view, start a new scene.
10. **Chapter endings.** Try to end on a question, a threat or a turn rather than on a character falling asleep.
11. **Descriptions of women.** Describe Sanchez the way she would see herself on a mission (the gown as a tool, the scarf as cover), not as a man's eye would catalogue her.
12. **Facts.** Before you name a real place, route, airport, law or brand, check it on a map or a website. Thriller readers will.
