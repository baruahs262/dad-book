# Structure and Flow Plan

*The flow part of your editorial package*

---

Dear Author,

This part of the package is about structure, not sentences. It covers the order your chapters come in, which scenes carry the book, which ones slow it down, and which ones are missing. The line edit and the continuity notes deal with words, dates and facts. They leave every chapter where you put it. This document is where we move things.

Everything below keeps your plot, your characters and your ending. Nothing here asks you to write a different book. The aim is for the book you have already written to read the way you imagined it.

**How to read the chapter numbers.** "Ch 12" always means the chapter as it is numbered in your current manuscript. "New 7" means a chapter in the proposed revised order. The full mapping between the two is in Section 2.

**Word counts** come from your manuscript as it stands: 82,130 words of text in 85 chapters.

## 1. How the book flows now

### The reading experience, part by part

**Part I (Ch 1–10).** The premise arrives fast and it is strong: a girl at a window, then four years later a young woman talking her way to a poker table and robbing the man across it. The poker room, her engineered scene at the door, her misreading of Alberto and then her correct read, and the climb down the façade are the best opening material in the book. Momentum stalls in three places.
- Ch 4 tells us a man is following her, which takes the shock out of Ch 5's "we are being followed".
- Ch 6 and Ch 7 are one morning split in two. Benedikt reconstructs her escape step by step, but the reader already watched every step in Ch 3, so his deductions are not discoveries.
- Ch 10 (alarm clock, shower, breakfast, an on-time flight) closes the first act on "rethink their go-forward plan". Ch 9's secret phone call about TransPacific is a far better curtain, and it is sitting one chapter earlier.

**Part II (Ch 11–18).** The jump back to 2012 comes just as the chase warms up, and the Part opens on 450 words of summary (Ch 11). Ch 12–13 then spend nearly 4,000 words on a job application: a full cover letter, a full acceptance email and a 240-word customer-service answer. The Part comes alive at John Patterson's door ("Let us take a walk instead") and at Bradley's turn ("I have no interest in why you are sniffing around. However, I am interested in you."). But its dramatic payoff, planning the heist with Bradley, is covered in one sentence. So the Part ends by pointing at Part I rather than giving us the scene that built it.

**Part III (Ch 19–36).** This Part has your best structural idea, the three-way chase: the siblings running, Maxwell shadowing them and Benedikt hunting them all. It also has the most confusing stretch in the book.
- Ch 19 replays the Zurich arrival from Maxwell's side without telling the reader it is a replay, and the pursuing car has changed.
- Ch 22 (Maxwell at the airport, 5 AM) comes after Ch 21 (Benedikt, 7 AM).
- Ch 10 has already shown the same flight at a different time.
- In Lisbon, one airport arrival is told three times and one jet landing twice.
- Ch 32 is dated the day before the chapter it follows.
- Roughly 3,500 words of travelogue (City Hall, the seven skirts, fado, the restaurant view, the farmhouse tour, lane-by-lane directions to Vale da Amoreira) pass with nothing threatening the heroes.

Then Ch 33–39 is a genuine 90-minute action sequence: Adriana's house, the break-in, two fights, and Sanchez shot and ordering Stephen to leave her. It is spread across eight short chapters and five viewpoints, and the "PART IV" divider sits in the middle of a gunpoint scene.

**Part IV (Ch 37–53).** The action peak (Ch 39, Stephen watching his sister shrink in the rear-view mirror) is followed by the book's central turn: Sanchez, wounded on Benedikt's plane, decides to play his heart as her way out. That is your most distinctive and marketable idea. The surrounding pages undercut it in three ways.
- Maxwell reaches an empty room four times (the warehouse, the hospital, the old house, the airfield), and each repeat weakens the next.
- Benedikt's longing is re-announced in nearly every chapter in the same words.
- The Part ends on two long spoken monologues. In Ch 50, Sanchez tells her story over lunch for about 2,000 words. In Ch 53, Maxwell explains himself for about 1,300 words, half of it retelling what we saw.

**Part V (Ch 54–85).** This is one Part of 32 chapters and 21,000 words, which is really two movements.

*The ship (Ch 54–71).* It contains the book's first true espionage set piece: the pen planted but never switched on, and the camera sliding out of a stack of papers while Benedikt is on the phone. It also has a fine moral chain: the medevac, then Finch's shame, then Finch's letter. Around these sit seven rounds of sleepless brooding, four near-identical passages of Benedikt reproaching himself, and three confrontations about her father that don't escalate.

*The border (Ch 72–85).* Pace is good through Vitebsk: Benedikt's "a long stroll to Noŭka" line is one of the best moments in the book. Then the ending thins out.
- Albert, the only witness to Daniel's years in the east, gets 489 words and explains nothing.
- Sanchez forgives "I told the Russians to get rid of him" overnight, off the page.
- The climax, the hostage ruse at Liozna, is a terrific idea. But it is fought against her own brother and the police, not against the villains.
- The rescue of her father, the goal of the whole novel, happens off the page in one sentence.
- Kosovo orders a tail that never appears. The mother never appears.
- The last paragraph reads like a trailer for a sequel.

### Where readers get lost

- **Time.** Datelines run backwards or contradict the text: Ch 22 before Ch 21, Ch 32 a day early, Ch 43 before Ch 42, Ch 82 before Ch 81, and two weeks passing between breakfast and 10 AM in Ch 78. The continuity notes fix the dates. This plan fixes the order.
- **Retold scenes.** Her escape is told three times (Ch 3, 4, 7), the Zurich arrival twice (Ch 5/8, 19), the flight twice (Ch 10, 22), the Lisbon arrival three times (Ch 24, 25, 28), the Montijo landing twice (Ch 28, 30), the shooting twice (Ch 39, 40), her backstory in Ch 50, and Maxwell's pursuit in Ch 53.
- **Viewpoint swaps inside scenes.** The worst are on the plane (Ch 47), in Maxwell's call to his office (Ch 48), in the interrogation of William (Ch 43), and in the siblings' car in Zurich (Ch 8). Ch 47 matters most, because the scene depends on the reader not knowing whether Sanchez's softening is real.

### The shape of the climax

As it stands, the book's emotional climax (Ch 83) and its plot climax (the rescue, Ch 85) are in different places. Only the first is on the page. The antagonists (Evan Slender, Kosovo, the Russians) never face the heroine. The central mystery set up in Ch 1 and Part II (what Daniel carried, why he was taken, why he was kept alive) is never answered. **The single biggest improvement you can make is to put the rescue and the answer on the page, in the same sequence, with the villains' tail closing in.**

## 2. Proposed chapter map

### The main decisions

**Sixty chapters in six Parts.** Most merges join two chapters that cover the same hour from the same side, or that split one scene in two. The book's rhythm stays the same, cross-cut short chapters with datelines, but each chapter now earns its header. The average length rises from about 970 words to about 1,380, which is where fast commercial thrillers like *Alias Emma* sit.

**Part II stays a single block. It does not interleave with the chase.** I considered interleaving the Ayr chapters through Parts III and IV, which is a common thriller device, and I recommend against it for this book, for four reasons.
1. **Your biggest problem is time, not pace.** Readers are already tracking five viewpoints, six countries and several deliberate overlaps. A third timeline threaded through the Lisbon cross-cut would multiply the confusion we are trying to remove.
2. **The handover between Part I and Part II is the best transition in the book.** Benedikt asks, in secret, whether the girl has any link to TransPacific. The next page puts her in the TransPacific lobby two years earlier. Interleaving would break that.
3. **Part II is one continuous investigation with its own arc**: a name, a door, a threat, a boss, a trap. Split into fragments, it would lose its build.
4. **Once the new heist-planning scene is written, Part II ends by delivering the reader straight back to the poker room.** They will understand Part I in a new light at exactly the moment Part III begins.

The price of keeping the block is that Part II must be shorter at the front, open in a scene rather than a summary, and carry its own danger. The map below does that. If an agent later asks for interleaving, the revised Ayr chapters will be self-contained and can be moved without rewriting.

**The Part dividers move to match the journey.**
- Part One (Girbin and Helsingør to Zurich) now ends on Benedikt's call about TransPacific.
- Part Two (Ayr) is unchanged in placement.
- Part Three (Zurich to Lisbon to Nazaré) now ends on Stephen driving away from his wounded sister, the strongest curtain you have.
- Part Four (the Captive: Lisbon to Tallinn) opens on Stephen's shame, with a stranger flagging him down.
- Part Five (the ship: Tallinn to Gdynia to Warsaw) is unchanged at its start.
- **A new Part Six** (the Border: Vitebsk, Smolensk, Liozna) begins with the flight into Belarus. The current Part V is too long to hold both movements.

Simple Part titles taken from the route would help the reader know where they are. This is optional.

**Ch 78's "two weeks".** I recommend the shorter wait: Benedikt wins his superiors round in a day. Two unexplained weeks of Sanchez waiting in Vitebsk would drain the tension just before the climax. The continuity notes give the alternative datelines for this (2–4 May). *Note: the tracked manuscript keeps your "two weeks" and dates Ch 78–85 to 15–17 May, because a line edit shouldn't cut your story time. If you take this recommendation, change the phrase and use the 2–4 May dates instead.*

## 3. Scenes to add

These are the scenes the reports found missing: events your plot already depends on that happen off the page. Each brief gives the job the scene does. The prose is yours to write.

**A. Ch 1 ending: the father doesn't come back (about 200 words, in new 1).**
After the window game, she waits in bed for his footsteps. The church bell rings for mass, the coffee is never made, and she goes back to the window and wipes it again with her sleeve. The street is ordinary, the bakery is open, and the black Range Rover is gone. Something small and wrong is on the pavement: the bakery bag, or the muffins. End there, with no narrator warning. The reader must see the absence for themselves.

**B. A later encounter with John Patterson (about 500 words, in new 9).**
Replace "for the next several weeks she stalked him" with one of those weeks, dramatised. She follows him after his shift to a depot yard or a pub and watches him meet someone who is clearly from head office. John sees her across the room, and his fear is now visible to someone else. This gives Part II real danger inside TransPacific, and it makes his later surrender (new 10) feel forced out of him rather than handed over.

**C. One training session with Bradley (about 400 words, in new 12).**
The year of training is one line at present. Show one session. He times her on a climbing wall, or has her go up a drainpipe at the back of a warehouse, and says nothing when she succeeds. This seeds the façade climb in Ch 3 and shows the reader the man before he disappears. Give him a habit that Maxwell repeats later: he always takes the seat in the corner facing the door. The attentive reader will feel it in the Zurich café and understand it in Tallinn.

**D. Planning the heist with Bradley (about 1,500 words: new 13, end of Part Two).**
*Early 2014, Ayr. Consider dating it 14 February, four years to the day since her father vanished.* Bradley gives her the target: the April tournament at Helsingør, Benedikt's habits, the suite, the guards, and the fact that the quarterly route map travels in his briefcase. The honey trap is *her* idea, and Bradley's silence when she says it should be something Maxwell has to answer for in Tallinn. She asks who he really is, and he won't say. She keeps the escape beyond the hotel to herself: "That part is mine." That single line explains both how the watcher knew her route out of the hotel and why he expected to follow her at Basel, not Zurich. The scene ends with her alone, dialling her brother: "I need a driver in Switzerland." This brings Stephen into the story and hands the reader straight back to the poker room.

**E. Maxwell's sting under the van (about 200 words, in new 19).**
When the documents spill in the cab, one sheet drifts under the van to where Maxwell lies. It carries a Nazaré address. That night he sends his police-officer friend to watch the house. It turns the bugging from a set piece into a plot engine, answers how Maxwell knew about Adriana, and sets both hunters on the same door.

**F. Benedikt's price for taking her aboard (about 400 words, in new 34).**
Taking a thief onto a trafficking voyage should cost him something we can see. A short call with his superior, in which he lies smoothly about who she is, or a word with the ship's captain, in which he stakes his own standing on her, would do it. Sanchez either overhears it or sees its effect. This is what makes the captive-to-ally turn feel earned rather than convenient.

**G. Maxwell's reason, and the kit (about 500 words, in new 35).**
Maxwell must give one reason he cannot just call in the police. For example: the microphone in Evan's vase was never authorised, nothing he holds is admissible, and he has no power abroad. That is what makes her going aboard necessary rather than stubborn. He should also acknowledge, in a line, what he did to an eighteen-year-old intern. End the chapter with the spy kit pressed into her hand as she leaves. It replaces the later flashback and gives the chapter an ending.

**H. The overheard enquiry (about 300 words, end of new 37).**
You already tell us that Benedikt "tried making a few calls" to trace her father. Put one on the page. She passes his door and hears him asking someone, carefully, about "a Scottish driver" from years ago. Then he sees her in the doorway and ends the call. This answers the photograph cliffhanger instead of letting it fade.

**I. The crate at Gdynia (about 600 words, in new 48).**
This is the moment the book finally shows *what* TransPacific smuggles, and at present it is reported in one retrospective paragraph. Dramatise it: the torn wrapping, a gilded face in torchlight, her body camera, and Benedikt's voice behind her asking what she is looking at. ## 5. Opening and ending

### The first five pages

An agent will read about 1,300 words: your Chapter 1 and the first page of Chapter 2. At present those pages contain:
- a wrong dateline;
- a paragraph of weather in the most ornate prose in the book ("a tincture passing through the morning haar");
- a Range Rover that becomes a Land Rover;
- a paragraph of summarised family virtue;
- a narrator's warning in place of an event;
- then "Stockholm, Denmark";
- a man introduced as a "Caucasian male";
- a heroine described by her breasts and her age ("late teens").

An agent who stops at page three will never reach the poker room.

What to do:

1. **Chapter 1 must end on an event, not a warning.** Keep the haar (it is a possible title motif) and the streetlights in two or three sentences. Make the Range Rover specific and wrong: the engine running, nobody getting out. Replace the family-virtue paragraph with one line each placing her mother and Stephen asleep in the house. That also makes her "Mom, dad, brother and me" true later. Then **keep the window game almost exactly as it is**: the sleeve stretched over her hand to clean the haze, and her waiting for her father "to dissolve in the morning mist". That is the heart of your book. Then scene A: she waits, he doesn't come, she goes back to the window, and the car is gone. Make her sixteen, with the date moved to 8:00 AM, and cut "little they knew … Change for the worse".

2. **Chapter 2 should open on her, not the limousine.** She is in the coffee shop watching the lifts, in a gown chosen for what it *hides*: the kit sewn into its lining. The arrival of the man she is waiting for can be two sentences seen through her eyes. Get to the door, and "Is this how you treat a lady?", by the fourth page. She is twenty; the text needn't say so, but it must never say "late teens".

3. **Link the girl and the woman without explaining.** One small echo would do it. When the café window mists, or later on the train, she wipes a pane clear with her sleeve. A reader who has just read Chapter 1 will know who she is, and why she is there, without being told.

### The final chapter

As written, the rescue happens off the page and Sanchez sends her father away alone within minutes of getting him back. The last paragraph (Benedikt "hopeful … soon she would accept his love", and "a few hurdles to cross") undercuts the "Yes, us" three lines earlier and reads as a sequel teaser. The rebuilt ending runs across three chapters (new 58–60, see Section 3). The final one, new 60, should do five things.

1. **The family beat.** Daniel's first question when he is safe should be about his wife. Stephen, or Maxwell's phone, puts her mother's voice in his ear, and Sanchez hears her mother's voice break. That is the moment she understands what she is about to give up. It costs her something visible. At present nothing does.

2. **"San".** Daniel should call her by the name only he used. If you plant his line "one day I will take you to all those places" (Ch 50) carefully, it can come back here, darkly true: she has now been to all of them.

3. **The crossing.** Keep your own line for Daniel: "Don't make me wait for long this time." It reverses the window game: now he is the one who will wait for her. Keep the image you already have, of him walking away "disappearing along the road as it bent away from her vision", towards Stephen, who is small at the far end.

4. **"Yes, us."** Keep the exchange in the car. It is earned now, by the night in Russia and by her reasoning in Vitebsk. Cut Benedikt's "hopeful" line and the "hurdles" paragraph.

5. **The last image is the window.** As the car pulls away, the rear window is misted. She wipes it with her sleeve, as she did at sixteen, and looks back at the empty bend in the road. In Chapter 1 she watched her father leave and he never came back. Now she is the one leaving, and she knows he will reach home. End there, on the image and not on an explanation. It also answers Stephen watching her shrink in the mirror at the end of Part Three.

Do not add an epilogue. If you feel the need to show Girbin, the mother and the house, that is a sign the crossing has not yet done its work.

## 6. Viewpoint and chapter headings

### One viewpoint per scene

Your narrator has a voice, and you should keep it: the storyteller's occasional wink, the "for reasons best known to her", the love of places. That is not the problem. The problem is whose *feelings* we are inside. Many scenes currently tell us what three or four people feel, including passers-by, receptionists, the doctor and the man being interrogated. The rule for the revision is simple.

- **One viewpoint character per scene.** Name that character in the chapter's first sentence. Other people's feelings are shown from outside: a face falls, a hand stops.
- **To change viewpoint, use a scene break** (a centred ornament and a line of white space). Never change it mid-paragraph.
- **Main viewpoints:** Sanchez (about half the book), Benedikt and Maxwell.
- **Secondary:** Stephen, in a few scenes: the Zurich street check, the road after the shooting, the waiting at Liozna.
- **Guest viewpoints**, one scene each and only when they carry something no main character can see: Francis at the airfield, Marty in the bunker, Kosovo setting the trap. Damian, William, the receptionist and the switchboard lady lose theirs.
- **Scenes to fix first**, because the head-hopping costs suspense:
  - the jet (new 31), where the reader must not know whether her softening is real;
  - Maxwell's call to Drake (new 30);
  - William's interrogation (new 27);
  - the siblings' car in Zurich (new 5);
  - the Tallinn breakfast and lunch (new 32), which may alternate between the two lovers, but only at scene breaks.
- **Cut the "little they knew" formula** (Ch 1, Ch 7). The structure now supplies the dramatic irony the narrator was announcing.

### Datelines

Keep them. With six countries, three hunters and several deliberate overlaps, they are the reader's map. But they must be trustworthy.

- **One dateline per chapter**, in one fixed format throughout, for example "7:00 PM, Monday 14th April 2014, Helsingør, Denmark". The weekday helps, and it matters on Good Friday. Your copy-editor will set the house style. Your job is consistency.
- **It must match the text.** If the dateline says 4:30 PM, nobody has dinner, and nothing is dark before sunset.
- **It names where the viewpoint character is**, not where the story is heading.
- **If a scene break inside a chapter moves to another place**, give that scene a short sub-heading ("Smolensk, 10:15 AM").
- **Deliberate rewinds are signalled twice**: by the dateline and by the first sentence. The opening of Part Three (Maxwell on the Zurich platform, at the same minute as new 5) is the model.
- **Part Two's headers** carry the date and place without a clock, as you already do, and the Part title should say the year.

### Time zones

- **Local time at the place in the dateline, always.** Scandinavia and Switzerland are one hour ahead of Portugal. The Baltic states and Belarus are one hour ahead of Scandinavia.
- **In the air**, use the time zone of departure ("11:30 PM, in the air, en route to Tallinn").
- **On the phone across zones**, the narration uses the viewpoint character's local time. When the gap matters to the plot, say both once ("ten in Lisbon, eleven in Zurich").

## 7. Revision order

Do the work in this order so that nothing you polish is later cut. Times assume about ten hours a week.

1. **Decide, and write it down (1–2 weeks).** A one-page story bible covering:
   - Sanchez 16 / 18 / 20;
   - what Daniel carried in 2010, where he hid it, and why he was kept alive;
   - who handed Albert's note;
   - what Maxwell is officially, and why he can't use the police;
   - the one-day wait at Smolensk;
   - the master calendar from the continuity notes.

   Every later step depends on these answers.

2. **Accept the fact and date fixes (1–2 weeks).** Work through the continuity and line-edit notes that settle geography, datelines, age and the poker hands. Do not spend time on line-level changes in passages this plan cuts.

3. **Write the ending first (7–9 weeks).** That means Albert, the night of reasoning, the plan and the pistol, the ambush from Sanchez's side, the night in Russia, Kosovo's trap, the extraction and the crossing (scenes K–R). What Daniel knows decides what must be planted earlier, so this comes before the fixes further back.

4. **Write Part Two's new scenes (3 weeks).** John's second encounter, the training session and the heist planning (B, C, D). These plant Maxwell's habits and explain the route.

5. **Cut, merge and reorder, Part by Part (6–8 weeks).** Follow the chapter map. Start with Part Three, which has the biggest savings and the most reordering, then Parts Four, Five and One. Write the remaining new scenes (E–J) as you reach them.

6. **Plant and pay off (2 weeks).** One pass with a checklist:
   - the pistol;
   - the camera Benedikt saw;
   - the Nazaré document;
   - the officer's name;
   - the corner seat;
   - "San";
   - the mother;
   - the sleeve on the window;
   - DAZZLE or the haar, if either becomes the title;
   - Adriana's "ill-fated night": pay it off or cut it.

7. **Rewrite the first five pages (1–2 weeks).** Do this now, once you know exactly what the last page answers.

8. **Viewpoint and dateline pass (2–3 weeks).** One viewpoint per scene. Check every header against its text.

9. **Line pass on the new material (3–4 weeks).** Your new scenes will need the same care the line edit gave the old ones: the same watch for near-miss words, "blurted", and emotions named rather than shown. Read them aloud beside an edited chapter to be sure they sound like the same writer on the same good day.

10. **Read the whole book straight through, then two readers (3–4 weeks).** One reader should know thrillers and one should not. Ask each the same three questions: where did you put it down, where were you confused about time, and did you believe "Yes, us"?

**Total: about 30–38 weeks at ten hours a week, so plan on seven to nine months.** That is a normal length for a structural revision of a debut, and it is time well spent. The book you will send out is the one the reports kept glimpsing: a heist, a three-way chase, a captor and a captive falling for each other, and a daughter who brings her father home, told in a voice that is unmistakably yours.
