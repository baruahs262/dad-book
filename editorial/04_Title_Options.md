# Title, Positioning and Pitch Memo

Working title: *The Last Scintilla*. Manuscript: about 82,000 words, 85 chapters, 5 parts.
Prepared from: chapters 1–2 and 85 of `book.md`, the S01–S09 developmental reads, and web checks run on 23 September 2026. Sources are listed at the end.

---

## 1. Why "The Last Scintilla" is weak for this market

1. **The book never earns the title.** "Scintilla" appears once, in Benedikt's chapter 9 report: "There was no scintilla of her existence." Chapter 26 also has two passing uses of "scintillating" (views). Nothing in the story is "the last scintilla", so nothing pays the title off. An agent will look for the meaning and won't find one.
2. **It fails the spoken test.** Many readers can't define, pronounce or spell *scintilla* ("scintila", "sintilla"). A thriller title gets said aloud by agents in acquisitions meetings, by booksellers and by readers recommending it. A title that people hesitate over, and can't type into a search box, loses sales it should have won.
3. **It signals the wrong shelf.** "The Last [rare Latinate noun]" sounds like literary fiction, fantasy or science fiction (a spark, a particle, an ending of the world). It doesn't suggest a spy chase, a honey-trap heist or a missing father. The "The Last …" pattern is also crowded in crime and thriller, so the only distinctive word is the obscure one.
4. **It repeats the manuscript's biggest risk.** Every developmental report flags ornate or misused vocabulary as the first thing an agent will notice. If the title is a thesaurus word and the first paragraph offers "a tincture passing through the morning haar", the agent gets the same warning twice before the story starts.

### Checked and rejected (strong collisions)

- **Return to Sender.** Craig Johnson's Longmire novel *Return to Sender* (2025, NYT bestseller) is about a missing mail carrier. That's far too close.
- **The Big Blind.** Louise Wener's *The Big Blind* (2003) is about a daughter with a gift for poker who is hunting her missing father. The premise overlaps, so avoid it. Lavie Tidhar also has a poker novella with the title.
- **The Map Thief.** Heather Terrell's 2008 thriller and Michael Blanding's 2014 true-crime book both use it.
- **Dissolve.** This is too close to Blædel and Nordbo's *Dissolved* (2023), a thriller about a missing parent, and Nicholas Binge's *Dissolution* (2025).
- **The Honey Trap** and **The Custodian.** Each has four or more current thrillers with the title.
- **All In**, **Poker Face**, **Tell No One** and **The Long Con.** These are saturated or owned by a famous book or TV series.

### Top 3, ranked

1. **The Tell.** This is the most on-genre and the most flexible. It is anchored in the book's best set piece (the poker room, where she reads Alberto's tell after first misreading it) and in its central twist (Benedikt's face at the photograph). "Tell" also works as a verb, for the confessions the ending depends on. It's short, easy to say and easy to put on a cover. The collision with Amy Griffin's memoir is real but on a different shelf, and titles aren't protected. Mention it to an agent if asked, but it shouldn't stop the title.
2. **Dazzle.** It's distinctive and clear of thriller collisions, and it carries a clever second meaning: dazzle camouflage hides by confusing the eye, which is how Sanchez operates. The caveat is that DAZZLE is named only once (ch. 54). If chosen, the ship's name should come back three or four times, and one line (Maxwell's or Sanchez's) should explain dazzle camouflage.
3. **Last Seen in the Haar.** It carries the emotional engine: the father who dissolved into the morning mist, the image that opens and closes the book. It's clear of collisions and keeps the author's "Last". The caveat is that it could shelve the book as Scottish crime, when most of the novel is a pan-European chase. Choose it if the author wants the family story to lead.

Runner-up if the author prefers a family-first title: **The Deliveryman's Daughter**.

Whichever title is chosen, its motif should be seeded deliberately. The *haar* appears only once in the manuscript and *DAZZLE* only once. The poker tells already run through chapter 2 and pay off at chapter 55.

## 3. Genre, category and positioning

**Shelf.** This is an **international espionage/conspiracy thriller with a dangerous-romance thread and a family-mystery engine**. It's female-led, it opens with a heist and a honey trap, and it becomes a cross-Europe chase: Scandinavia, Zurich, Lisbon and Nazaré, Tallinn, the Baltic by ship, Poland, Belarus and the Russian border.
- UK retail and Thema: *Crime & Thriller > Spy & Espionage thrillers* (Thema FHD).
- US (BISAC): FICTION / Thrillers / Espionage (FIC006000), with Thrillers / Suspense as a secondary.
- It's commercial rather than upmarket.
- The romance (the hunter falls for the hunted, and the heroine finds that her feigned feelings are real) is a selling point for crossover readers, but it should be pitched as a thread, not as a genre. The book should not be queried as romantic suspense.
- It's set in 2014. The pitch doesn't need to hide this, but it's a period choice, and the agent should see it as one.

**Word count.** **82,000 is right.** Industry guidance puts the safe band for a thriller debut at about 80,000–90,000 words (Writer's Digest calls 80–89,999 "100% safe"), and a range up to about 100,000 is acceptable. The caution is the revision plan. The developmental reads recommend net cuts, which come to roughly 10,000–15,000 words once line-tightening is included:
- halve chapters 11–13;
- trim about 40% of the Lisbon/Nazaré travelogue;
- cut Maxwell's dead ends;
- take S07 from about 9,000 to 6,500 words and S08 from about 8,500 to 6,000.

They also call for additions: an on-page climax and extraction (ch. 85), a fuller Albert scene, and the Stockholm heist planning with Bradley. **The target after revision is 82,000–90,000 words, with a floor of about 78,000.** Put the words saved by the cuts into the climax and the missing dramatised scenes, not back into description.

**Comparable titles** (all verified; published 2018–2023; successful but not mega-sellers):

| Comp | Why it fits |
|---|---|
| **Ava Glass, *Alias Emma*** (Century/Penguin, 2022) | A young, competent British woman runs a covert operation on the move, with Russian menace and pace over introspection. It's in TV development. This is the closest UK tonal comp for the chase. |
| **Holly Watt, *To the Lions*** (Raven/Bloomsbury, 2019) | A British female lead uncovers an international conspiracy that the powerful profit from. A debut, it won the CWA Ian Fleming Steel Dagger. Good for showing the UK market buys this shape from a new author. |
| **Luke Jennings, *Codename Villanelle*** (John Murray, 2018) | A cat-and-mouse across Europe in which the hunter becomes obsessed with the hunted. This is the Benedikt–Sanchez engine. **Caution:** the *Killing Eve* TV series made it famous, so cite it for the hunter–hunted dynamic, not for sales. |
| **I. S. Berry, *The Peacock and the Sparrow*** (Atria 2023; Bedford Square UK 2024) | Espionage in which the operative's romance with someone on the wrong side becomes the moral trap. A debut, it won the Edgar and ITW Best First Novel awards and was a Times/Sunday Times Thriller of the Year. |
| **David McCloskey, *Moscow X*** (W. W. Norton, 2023) | Seduction, a financial heist and a Russian endgame, with a woman playing her own game inside the operation. |
| **Alma Katsu, *Red Widow*** (Putnam, 2021) | A female-led spy thriller that turns on the Russia division and personal entanglement. It's a US-market comp for the same readership. |

For a query letter, pair one UK and one US comp. For example: "*Alias Emma* meets *The Peacock and the Sparrow*" or "for readers of *Codename Villanelle* and *To the Lions*". Don't cite le Carré, *Red Sparrow* or *The Girl with the Dragon Tattoo*. They're too big and signal inexperience.

## 4. Draft query hook (about 150 words)

> Scotland, 2010: a deliveryman steps into the morning haar to fetch his daughter's Sunday muffins, and never comes home.
>
> Four years later, twenty-year-old Sanchez Roberto talks her way to a high-stakes poker table beside Benedikt, the man who guards the smuggling routes of logistics giant TransPacific, and offers to be his lady luck. By dawn she has drugged him, photographed his coded route map and climbed down the hotel's façade into the dark. Benedikt should want her dead. Instead, as he hunts her from Zurich to Lisbon, he finds he simply wants her. And someone else is shadowing them both.
>
> When a bullet in Portugal delivers Sanchez into Benedikt's hands, she turns captivity into cover, boarding the black-hulled DAZZLE to follow the route east toward Russia, where her father may still be alive. But to bring him home, she must decide how far to trust the man who helped make him disappear.

(About 150 words. It assumes the fixes: Sanchez is 20 in 2014, the Scandinavian venue is left unnamed so it works whichever geography fix is adopted, and the extraction happens on the page.)
