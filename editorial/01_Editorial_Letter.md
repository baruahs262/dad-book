## 1. The verdict

You asked me to be honest, so I will be plain.

**As first submitted, this manuscript would be declined by a literary agent or a major publisher, almost certainly within the first five pages.** That is not a verdict on the story. It is a verdict on the surface. Before the story has a chance, an agent meets:

- **Sentence-level English that signals "not ready."** Words used in the wrong sense ("never failed to *cease* her excitement", "a strong *rumor*", "*stature*" for posture, "*delirious*" for agitated), missing articles ("few feet away"), dropped possessives ("Benedikt turn"), and dialogue punctuation that is wrong more often than right. Agents read hundreds of samples a week. The first misused word makes them read more carefully. By the fifth they stop.
- **Facts a thriller reader will catch.** "Stockholm, Denmark" is in the second chapter heading. The poker hand in Chapter 2 has a straight beating a full house, and a straight flush built from cards the other player is holding. There is a passport queue on a flight inside the Schengen zone, and a military air base with a receptionist. Thriller readers forgive a great deal, but not a thriller that gets its own mechanics wrong.
- **A heroine whose age reads as under eighteen during the seduction scene.** Chapter 1 made her "in her early teens" in 2010, and Chapter 2 made her "in her late teens" in 2014, with a sexual scene in Chapter 3. In today's market that alone ends the read, whatever the author intended.

**The edit you now hold fixes nearly all of that surface.** Once you accept the tracked changes, the prose is correct, the facts are right, the timeline holds together and the heroine is twenty. What remains is recognisably your book, in your voice, only cleaner.

**It will not, by itself, get you a yes from a major house.** The deeper problems are structural, and no line edit can solve them:

1. **The ending happens off the page.** The rescue of Daniel, which the whole book has been building towards, is one sentence. The villains are never confronted. We never learn what happened to Daniel in 2010, what was in the package, or why he was kept alive. The romance resolves ("Yes, us") without Sanchez ever reckoning with Benedikt's confession that he told the Russians to get rid of her father.
2. **The middle is told more than shown.** There are long monologues (Sanchez's life story in Chapter 50, Maxwell's confession in Chapter 53), several scenes told two or three times from different points of view, and travel-guide passages in Lisbon, Nazaré and Tallinn where nothing threatens anyone.
3. **The point of view moves between heads inside a scene.** Readers lose track of whose scene it is, and suspense leaks away.
4. **Plot logic has gaps** that each need a sentence or a small scene: the unplanted sedative, the guns left in the field, the police officer's body nobody notices, Maxwell's legal authority abroad, and how a guarded captive is free by the afternoon.
5. **There are 85 short chapters**, many of them brief bridges, some out of time order. The book reads as a series of reports more than a rising line.

**My honest assessment after the edit:** the premise is commercial and the bones are sound. With a full structural revision along the lines of the attached plan, done over some months, this can be a credible debut thriller to query. Without that revision, even the cleaned manuscript would, I believe, earn polite rejections, and at best a few requests for pages on the strength of the premise. The market for debut thrillers is crowded. Agents take on very few new clients from thousands of queries. The book needs to be finished, not just correct.

I would rather tell you this now than let you spend your first round of queries on a draft that is not yet your best. You only get one first impression with each agent.

## 2. What works, and must be protected

These are not courtesies. They are the reasons I believe the book is worth revising.

- **The premise.** A daughter spends four years engineering a honey-trap heist against the man who guards her father's kidnappers. He sees through her decoy and falls for her anyway. It is high-concept, easy to pitch, and the kind of set-up thriller editors buy.
- **The window game.** In Chapter 1 the girl wipes the frost from her window to watch her father "dissolve in the morning mist", then races back to bed to pretend she is asleep. It is the emotional seed of the whole novel. It pays off when Daniel walks away round the bend at the very end. This is your best image. Build the book around it.
- **Sanchez engineering her own entrance.** She provokes the guards so that Benedikt has to rescue her ("She is with me"), then offers: "For tonight, let me be your lady luck." She reads Alberto's tell wrong the first time and right the second. That is character shown through action.
- **Benedikt seeing through the decoy theft.** A villain who is clever makes the heroine look better.
- **Strong set pieces:** the climb down the hotel façade, and the bird that nearly makes her fall; Maxwell clinging under the van; the dropped camera on the ship; the hostage bluff at the end ("His men had shot me once…").
- **The photograph.** Benedikt's face drains white when he sees her father's picture. That is the best turn in the second half. Everything around it should serve it.
- **Warmth.** Jules and his mother, Stephen's running hunger joke, and Albert's "He used to talk about you all the time." Your book has a heart, which many thrillers do not.
- **Your voice.** It is formal, earnest and old-fashioned in the best sense: a narrator who takes the reader by the hand. The edit keeps it. I did not try to make you sound like anyone else.

## 3. How the line edit works, and how to use it

### What we did
The manuscript was read in full several times, then edited paragraph by paragraph and reviewed a second time with fresh eyes. The editors first studied your style in detail: your vocabulary, sentence rhythm, dialogue habits, imagery and narrative voice. They then edited to a written style sheet built from that study (`06_House_Style_Sheet.md`). The rules were strict:

- **Delete before replacing.** Most problems were extra words, so most fixes are deletions.
- **When a word had to change, use a word you already use elsewhere in the book.**
- **No new images, no new metaphors, no new plot and no modern slang.** No em dashes, which you never use. No short "punch-line" sentences at the ends of paragraphs, which you rarely write.
- **Keep your formal dialogue:** "I am", "Let us", "Will you?"
- **Keep your sentence shapes:** same subject, same opening, same order.

About two-thirds of the paragraphs carry at least one change. The typical edited paragraph has about a tenth of its words changed. The book is about 4–5% shorter.

### Three kinds of red lines
Word lets you filter changes by reviewer (**Review → Show Markup → Specific People**). I used three reviewer names so you can deal with each kind in one go:

| Reviewer name | What it covers | Suggested approach |
|---|---|---|
| **Editor** | The line edit: grammar, word choice, punctuation, tightening, fact and timeline fixes | Go through these one by one. They are the heart of the edit. |
| **Editor (Spelling)** | Conversion to British spelling (colour, realise, centre, grey, tyres, Mum) | Accept all at once if you are submitting to UK publishers. Reject all if you are targeting American ones. |
| **Editor (Names)** | The character name changes (see section 6 below) | Review the list, then accept or reject as a set. |

**Margin comments** (headed *Query*, *Fact*, *Continuity*, *Cut*, *Suggestion* or *Note*) explain the larger changes, ask about passages whose meaning I could not be sure of, and flag problems that need your own rewriting. Please read every comment, even if you reject the change it describes.

**One caution.** Accept the dateline changes (the time and date lines under each chapter number) as a group, or reject them as a group. They were corrected together to make one consistent calendar (see `05_Continuity_and_Timeline.md`). Accepting some and not others will re-break the timeline.

### Changes I made that go beyond grammar
These are all tracked and explained in comments, so you can reverse any of them:

- **Stockholm → Helsingør.** Stockholm is in Sweden. Your text wants a small Danish town about 40 minutes by train from Copenhagen, with a grand old hotel and a casino. Helsingør fits exactly (the Hotel Marienlyst there has a casino). The Swedish slips (kronor, "the poker game in Sweden") are corrected to match.
- **Her age.** She is sixteen in 2010, eighteen and a first-year college student in Ayr in 2012, and about twenty in April 2014.
- **The poker hands** in Chapter 2 are rebuilt so the cards add up and the right man wins.
- **The calendar.** The Denmark chapters move forward two days, so the Zurich arrival falls the day before the Lisbon flight, as your later chapters require. The Part II dates now run in order. The last chapters move to mid-May to honour your own "a good two weeks". About half of the dateline headers change.
- **Sexualised description** of Sanchez in her first scene and in the love scene is trimmed by deletion only. The scene itself remains.
- **Wrong places and facts:** Vitebsk, Smolensk, Liozna, Gdynia and Paljassaare spellings; euros, not pesos; Portuguese, German and Danish phrases corrected; "Range Rover" throughout; "Sackgasse"; and others.

## 4. The big revision, ranked

The full plan is in `02_Structure_and_Flow_Plan.md`. In order of importance:

1. **Write the ending on the page.** Dramatise the extraction from the compound. Let Kozlov's men, who are ordered to follow Benedikt and then forgotten, close in at the border. Let Daniel recognise Benedikt, and let Daniel tell his story. Make Sanchez's final choice cost her something. End on the father walking round the bend, echoing the window in Chapter 1.
2. **Solve your own mystery.** Decide exactly what happened in Girbin in February 2010: what Daniel was carrying, what he saw, who took him, why the police went quiet, and why he was kept alive. Then plant the clues through the book, especially in Part II, so the ending pays off rather than explains.
3. **Earn the romance.** Benedikt is complicit in her father's disappearance. Give Sanchez one honest scene of reasoning before she forgives him. Give Benedikt a surname, a nationality and a consistent inner life: in his own chapters he cannot pretend not to know what he later confesses.
4. **Restructure for momentum.** Merge short bridge chapters, from 85 down to about 55–62. Cut the retellings. Put chapters in time order where the current order confuses (for example, Chapter 22 before Chapter 21). Move the Part IV divider, which currently falls in the middle of a gunpoint scene.
5. **One point of view per scene.** When you need to switch heads, use a scene break.
6. **Dramatise, don't summarise.** Halve Sanchez's Chapter 50 monologue and let Benedikt react inside it. Show the heist planning with Bradley, which is currently one sentence. Cut the travelogue by about 40%.
7. **Close the logic gaps** listed in the continuity file: the sedative, the gown, the guns in the field, the officer's body, the Schengen flight, Montijo, Maxwell's authority, the medevac, and the gadgets.
8. **Tie up dropped threads:** the mother, Adriana, the kidnapped doctor, William and Marty, and MacCallan.

## 5. Flow

Here is how the book reads now, part by part (details and a word budget are in the structure plan):

- **Part I (Helsingør to Zurich)** has the book's best hook: the poker room, the seduction, the heist, the climb. It then sags. The escape is told three times, and Part I ends on a quiet flight instead of Benedikt's chilling phone call about TransPacific. End it on that call.
- **Part II (Ayr, 2012)** is a nine-chapter flashback block that arrives just as the chase gets going, and it spends thousands of words on a job application. Compress it hard. Consider threading it through Parts I and III as short flashback chapters instead.
- **Part III (Zurich to Lisbon to Nazaré)** has a good cat-and-mouse design with three hunters, but the chapters run backwards in time and the travel description slows the middle.
- **Part IV (the Nazaré raid and the flight to Tallinn)** is the most exciting stretch, but it is cut into many very short chapters with rapid point-of-view changes, and it has the biggest plot holes.
- **Part V (Tallinn, the ship and Belarus)** opens with two long monologues, finds its footing on the ship with the photograph, the recorder and the camera, and then reaches an ending that is summarised rather than shown.

**The single biggest improvement to flow** is to let each chapter end on a question the next chapter answers, rather than on a character going to sleep or thinking over what has happened. About a quarter of your chapters currently end on sleep or reflection.

## 7. Title

*The Last Scintilla* is not working for you. The word "scintilla" appears once, in passing, and nothing in the story is "the last scintilla". Many readers cannot say or spell it, and it signals literary fiction or science fiction rather than a thriller.

My recommendations, in order:

1. **The Tell.** A poker tell is how Sanchez gets into Benedikt's world, and the book's turning point is Benedikt's own tell when he sees her father's photograph. It also carries the verb: this is a book about who will finally tell the truth. It is short, strong and on-genre. (There is a 2025 memoir with the same title, but on a different shelf.)
2. **Dazzle.** The black ship's name. Dazzle camouflage hides a ship by confusing the eye, which is exactly how Sanchez works. If you choose it, name the ship three or four more times.
3. **Last Seen in the Haar.** It holds the father who vanished into the Scottish sea mist, and keeps your word "Last". Choose it if you want the family story to lead.

Also worth considering: *The Deliveryman's Daughter*, *Sub Rosa* and *What the Haar Took*. Whatever you choose, plant its motif through the book. The full list, collision checks and reasoning are in `04_Title_Options.md`.

## 8. The market, and how to submit

- **Shelf:** an international espionage and conspiracy thriller, with a dangerous romance and a missing-father mystery driving it. Query it as a thriller, not as romantic suspense.
- **Length:** 82,000 words is right for a debut thriller. After revision, aim for 82,000–90,000: cut about 10–15,000 words of summary and travelogue, and spend them on the ending and the missing scenes.
- **Comparable titles** for your query letter (pick one UK and one US): Ava Glass, *Alias Emma*; Holly Watt, *To the Lions*; I. S. Berry, *The Peacock and the Sparrow*; David McCloskey, *Moscow X*.
- **A draft pitch paragraph** is in `04_Title_Options.md`. It is written for the revised book, with Sanchez at twenty and the ending on the page.
- **How to reach Penguin.** Penguin Random House, like most major publishers, generally accepts fiction only through literary agents. Some divisions occasionally run open-submission windows. Check each imprint's current guidelines before sending anything. In practice, the route to a Penguin imprint is a literary agent: research agents who represent spy thrillers, follow each agency's submission rules exactly, and send a query letter with the opening chapters.
- **Before you query:** do the structural revision, then have two or three trusted readers who read thrillers go through the revised book (not friends who will be kind). Then query in small batches of eight to ten agents, so you can learn from the response before you use up your list.

## 9. Suggested next steps

1. Read this letter and the comments in the tracked manuscript.
2. Accept or reject the **Spelling** and **Names** layers as sets.
3. Work through the **Editor** changes. Don't be shy about rejecting anything that does not sound like you: it is your book.
4. Decide the ending: what happened in 2010, and how the rescue plays out. Everything else in the revision depends on it.
5. Revise using `02_Structure_and_Flow_Plan.md`, in the order it suggests.
6. Run your new pages past the house style sheet (`06`). It lists your personal habits to watch for: "with a … tone", the -ness nouns, "blurted", and naming emotions instead of showing them.
7. Get beta readers, then query.

This is a book with a real story in it: a daughter who would not stop looking for her father. That story deserves to be told as well as it can be. I hope this edit gets you most of the way there.
